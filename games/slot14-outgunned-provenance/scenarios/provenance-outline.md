# PROVENANCE — outline

**Outgunned · the Barranco de Sant Grau, Aragón, Spain · October 1938 ·
5 players · 3h30**

---

## Overview

The Ebro front is collapsing, the roads north are full of people walking, and a
German survey column with Condor Legion papers has spent nine days in a dry
riverbed sixty kilometres behind the line, doing nothing anyone can see.

They are not digging anything up. They have brought something with them — a
twelfth-century reliquary, bought at auction in Vienna in 1932, with an export
licence, a bill of sale, and a provenance file three centimetres thick proving
it was looted from this valley by Napoleon's quartermasters in 1811.

They are putting it back. They are entirely within their rights. You have until
Thursday to stop them.

**Content warnings:** Nazism and the real violence of it; the Spanish Civil War,
including reprisal killings and refugee columns; religious desecration; pulp
gunplay and a truck chase. Outgunned characters do not die without their
player choosing it — but this scenario is set in a real atrocity and does not
pretend otherwise. Safety tools in use.

**Scaling:** written for **5**. At four, drop the pilot and start the crew
already in the valley. Nothing essential lives on one PC.

---

## Design intent

1. **The villains are legally and morally in the right, and that is the game.**
   Every instinct an adventure table has — *they're looting it, get it back* —
   is exactly inverted. They have better paperwork than you and a fairer claim,
   and they must be stopped anyway.
2. **The 1811 theft was the rescue.** The whole scenario is the players
   assembling that one sentence out of a provenance file, and then having to act
   on it with twenty minutes and no proof anyone would accept.
3. **Pulp register, real ground.** Trucks, fists, a rope bridge, a dynamite
   budget. But the valley has a name, the village has names, and the people
   walking north are not scenery.
4. **No wonder weapon.** The reliquary does nothing for whoever holds it. It
   has one function and it performs it in one place, and that is why they want
   it in that place.
5. **The climax is a hole in the ground and a decision about a document.**
   Act Three is five people, a niche in a rock face, and the question of what to
   do with an object nobody can safely own.

## Handling the material

The house constraints from the DEAD AIR bible still apply here, adapted for a
register where the villains can be punched:

1. **No wonder-weapon glamour.** The Ahnenerbe in this scenario are competent,
   well-funded and wrong. Nothing they have works better than it should.
2. **The Spanish dead are named.** The village of Sant Grau has a list, and the
   list is a handout.
3. **No uniforms or regalia as spectacle.** The survey column wears field kit
   and carries clipboards. Its menace is the authority behind it, not its
   tailoring.
4. **The war is not a backdrop for a treasure hunt.** The refugee column on the
   north road is in two scenes and the scenario does not use it for atmosphere.
5. **Nobody is secretly a good German.** Dr Ritter believes what he is doing is
   scholarship and restitution, and he is sincere, and that is worse.

## Player experience

You are in Spain for five different reasons and none of them was this. A priest
with no parish left shows you a photograph of a German lorry parked in a dry
riverbed and asks, politely, whether you would mind. By the second hour you have
the survey column's order of march, a rope bridge you should not have crossed,
and a file of documents that proves beyond argument that the Germans are the
rightful owners of the thing in the crate. By the third hour you have read the
1811 receipt properly — the one signed by a French quartermaster who did not
list the reliquary as plunder, but as *removed for safety, by order, and not to
be returned* — and worked out that he was not stealing it. By Thursday morning
you are standing over a niche cut into rock, holding a crate nobody can safely
own, with a column of lorries coming up the barranco.

## Plot summary — GM truth

The niche in the rock face above the Barranco de Sant Grau is not a shrine. It
is a **fitting**. The reliquary sits in it the way a key sits in a lock, and
what it opens is not a door.

For six hundred years the village understood the arrangement well enough to keep
the thing in it and leave it alone. In **1811** a French quartermaster named
**Hilaire Fournel** came through with Suchet's army, was told what the niche was
by a priest who had no better options, and removed the reliquary deliberately —
writing it up as a requisition because there was no form for what he was
actually doing. He sent it north through four sets of hands and it surfaced at a
Vienna auction house in 1874.

**Dr Albrecht Ritter**, 51, Ahnenerbe, bought it for the Reich in 1932 on the
strength of a genuinely excellent piece of scholarship: he reconstructed the
1811 route, matched the reliquary to the niche, and proved the object's origin
beyond reasonable doubt. He is a careful, serious, decent-mannered man and he is
about to open the lock, because he believes the niche is a reliquary station and
the restoration is an act of repair.

**What comes out** is the scenario's one piece of restraint. It is not described
until Act Three, it is not fought, and it does not negotiate. The scenario's
threat is the *opening*, not the thing — and the thing will go north with the
retreating army, which is the ending nobody wants.

**The village of Sant Grau** has known since 1811 and has said nothing, because
saying it brought the French. Forty-one people remain. **Rosalía Benach**, 74,
is the one who will talk, and she wants the same thing Ritter wants — the thing
back where it belongs — for the exact opposite reason, and she is the hardest
NPC in the scenario to argue with.

## Structure

| Phase | Clock | Content | GM checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Introduction** | 0:00–0:15 | Safety line and the note on the material. Pregens, Roles, Tropes, and why each of you is in Spain. The priest and the photograph. | Everyone has said why they are in Aragón. |
| **Act One** — *the column* | 0:15–1:15 | The road north. The barranco from above. The scaffolding, the crate, the order of march. First contact with the column — papers, politeness, and a clipboard. | The table knows the Germans are legal and has seen the crate. |
| *break* | 10 min | On the export licence. | |
| **Act Two** — *the file* | 1:25–2:55 | Sant Grau and Rosalía Benach. The provenance file, obtained by whatever means the table enjoys. **The 1811 receipt, and its reverse.** Splits into the village (Track A) and the column's camp (Track B). The truck chase sits here. | Before 2:55: the table has read Fournel's note and knows Thursday is the deadline. |
| **Act Three** — *the niche* | 2:55–3:20 | First light, Thursday. No new information. Five people, a crate, a hole in a rock face, and lorries coming up the barranco. | A decision was made out loud by a named player. |
| **Epilogue** | 3:20–3:35 | Per-PC vignettes keyed to the ending. Sant Grau's list is the last line. | |

**Hard rule:** at 2:45 on the clock, go to Act Three whatever is unfinished.

**Act Two pacing note:** if the clock has beaten the table, Rosalía says it
straight — her grandmother was told by the priest who told Fournel, the niche is
a lock, and the Germans are three days from turning the key. She has no proof
and the table need not believe her; she exists so Act Three is never
unreachable.

### Endings (all four written)

1. **Buried elsewhere.** The reliquary goes somewhere else and stays there. It
   is now your problem, permanently, and the epilogue says so.
2. **Destroyed.** It can be destroyed. The scenario does not say what that costs
   until someone tries it, and then it says so plainly.
3. **Restored.** Ritter puts it back, on schedule, with the correct ceremony.
   Written as a real ending and played straight — the epilogue is the village,
   not a monster.
4. **Taken north.** Somebody decides the thing is worth more than the valley.
   Also written straight.

**If it becomes a straight firefight with the column:** it can, it is Outgunned,
and it is survivable. The scaffolding is still up on Thursday and Ritter has a
deputy with the same schedule and less patience.

## Handouts

1. **The provenance file** — bill of sale, 1932 export licence, auction
   catalogue entry 1874. Immaculate, and entirely genuine.
2. **The 1811 requisition**, front: a quartermaster's inventory line.
3. **The same document, reverse** — Fournel's note. The scenario turns here.
4. **Sant Grau's list** — forty-one names.
5. **The survey column's order of march** and the Thursday schedule.
6. **A map of the barranco**, with the scaffolding marked.

## Open questions

1. **What comes out of the niche, and how much is ever shown?** Current call:
   described only by effect, never statted, and the scenario ends before it is
   resolved. Needs a playtest to see whether a table finds that satisfying or
   cheated.
2. Is Ritter killable in Act Two? Leaning yes, and the restoration still happens
   on Thursday under Degen, which is the point.
3. **Does the table get the 1811 reverse, or earn it?** Leaning earn — but it is
   the hinge of the whole scenario, so it needs a written fallback and the
   Countdown must deliver it.
4. How much Spanish Civil War does a four-hour pulp game carry before the tone
   breaks? Watch it at the first playtest; the refugee column is the test.
5. **Outgunned's Death Roulette needs explaining in under ninety seconds** at a
   con table. Write the script, do not improvise it.
