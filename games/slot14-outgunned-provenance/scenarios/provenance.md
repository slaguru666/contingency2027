### Design intent (read this first)

1. **The villains are legally and morally in the right, and that is the game.**
   Every instinct an adventure table has — *they're looting it, get it back* —
   is exactly inverted. They have better paperwork than you and a fairer claim,
   and they must be stopped anyway.
2. **The 1811 theft was the rescue.** The whole scenario is the players
   assembling that one sentence out of a provenance file, and then having to act
   on it with twenty minutes and no proof anyone would accept.
3. **Pulp register, real ground.** Trucks, fists, a rope bridge, a dynamite
   budget. But the valley has a name, the village has names, and the people
   walking north are not scenery.
4. **No wonder weapon.** The reliquary does nothing for whoever holds it. It
   has one function and it performs it in one place, and that is why they want
   it in that place.
5. **The climax is a hole in the ground and a decision about a document.**
   Act Three is five people, a niche in a rock face, and the question of what to
   do with an object nobody can safely own.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

The niche in the rock face above the Barranco de Sant Grau is not a shrine. It
is a **fitting**. The reliquary sits in it the way a key sits in a lock, and
what it opens is not a door.

For six hundred years the village understood the arrangement well enough to keep
the thing in it and leave it alone. In **1811** a French quartermaster named
**Hilaire Fournel** came through with Suchet's army, was told what the niche was
by a priest who had no better options, and removed the reliquary deliberately —
writing it up as a requisition because there was no form for what he was
actually doing. He sent it north through four sets of hands and it surfaced at a
Vienna auction house in 1874.

**Dr Albrecht Ritter**, 51, Ahnenerbe, bought it for the Reich in 1932 on the
strength of a genuinely excellent piece of scholarship: he reconstructed the
1811 route, matched the reliquary to the niche, and proved the object's origin
beyond reasonable doubt. He is a careful, serious, decent-mannered man and he is
about to open the lock, because he believes the niche is a reliquary station and
the restoration is an act of repair.

**What comes out** is the scenario's one piece of restraint. It is not described
until Act Three, it is not fought, and it does not negotiate. The scenario's
threat is the *opening*, not the thing — and the thing will go north with the
retreating army, which is the ending nobody wants.

**The village of Sant Grau** has known since 1811 and has said nothing, because
saying it brought the French. Forty-one people remain. **Rosalía Benach**, 74,
is the one who will talk, and she wants the same thing Ritter wants — the thing
back where it belongs — for the exact opposite reason, and she is the hardest
NPC in the scenario to argue with.

## Player Experience

You are in Spain for five different reasons and none of them was this. A priest
with no parish left shows you a photograph of a German lorry parked in a dry
riverbed and asks, politely, whether you would mind. By the second hour you have
the survey column's order of march, a rope bridge you should not have crossed,
and a file of documents that proves beyond argument that the Germans are the
rightful owners of the thing in the crate. By the third hour you have read the
1811 receipt properly — the one signed by a French quartermaster who did not
list the reliquary as plunder, but as *removed for safety, by order, and not to
be returned* — and worked out that he was not stealing it. By Thursday morning
you are standing over a niche cut into rock, holding a crate nobody can safely
own, with a column of lorries coming up the barranco.

## Open questions (carried from the outline — resolve or delete)

1. **What comes out of the niche, and how much is ever shown?** Current call:
   described only by effect, never statted, and the scenario ends before it is
   resolved. Needs a playtest to see whether a table finds that satisfying or
   cheated.
2. Is Ritter killable in Act Two? Leaning yes, and the restoration still happens
   on Thursday under Degen, which is the point.
3. **Does the table get the 1811 reverse, or earn it?** Leaning earn — but it is
   the hinge of the whole scenario, so it needs a written fallback and the
   Countdown must deliver it.
4. How much Spanish Civil War does a four-hour pulp game carry before the tone
   breaks? Watch it at the first playtest; the refugee column is the test.
5. **Outgunned's Death Roulette needs explaining in under ninety seconds** at a
   con table. Write the script, do not improvise it.
