## GM ESSENTIALS (one glance, any GM)

- **System crash-course (Outgunned Adventure 1.1):**
  - **Dice pool = Attribute + Skill** in d6. *"A roll's dice pool = Attribute +
    Skill. Each Skill is listed under its usual Attribute, but any Skill can be
    used with any Attribute"* (p. 50 of the transcription set; book Section I).
    **Say that second half out loud at the table** — it is how a party of six
    non-gunfighters gets to act in a pulp game.
  - **Successes are matching numbers.** Two of a kind = **Basic**, three =
    **Critical**, four = **Extreme**, five = **Impossible**.
    `[unverified — the four difficulty names are confirmed, and so is the Grit
    ladder keyed to them; the number-of-matches mapping and the sixth tier
    (Jackpot) are from memory, not from the transcription. Check one page
    before the first playtest.]`
  - **Re-roll, then All In.** Keep your matches, re-roll the rest. Feats grant
    **Free Re-rolls**. **Luck** = +1 to a roll you deem important (p. 72).
    **Spotlight** = once per character, it simply works.
  - **Grit is 12 boxes.** A failed **Dangerous Roll** fills boxes by
    difficulty: **Basic 1 · Critical 3 · Extreme 9 · Impossible all**.
    **Damage Control:** each Basic Success you *did* score avoids 1 box, each
    Critical avoids 3 — never on an Impossible roll. The **12th box is the Hot
    Box: filling it immediately gives 2 Luck.**
  - **Nobody dies by accident.** See **THE DEATH ROULETTE SCRIPT** below.
- **Mechanics inline:** `[OG: Attribute+Skill — what it does]`
- **The clock counts play time** — pause it for the break.
- **Hard rule: at 2:45, go to Act Three** whatever is unfinished.
- **The villains are right.** They have better paperwork and a fairer claim
  than anyone at the table, and they must be stopped anyway. Do not give the
  GM's voice to the counter-argument; let the players find it.
- **Safety:** Nazism and the real violence of it; the Spanish Civil War,
  including reprisal killings and refugee columns; religious desecration; pulp
  gunplay. **Set in a real atrocity and does not pretend otherwise.** Name the
  safety tool at seating — 30 seconds, no ceremony.

### THE DEATH ROULETTE SCRIPT — read this, do not improvise it

*Ninety seconds, during seating, once. The outline flags this as a required
script and it is.*

> *"You will not die in this game unless you choose to. Here is how that works.
> You have twelve Grit boxes. When you fail something dangerous you fill boxes
> in — one, three, or nine, depending on how hard the thing was. When all twelve
> are full, you are not dead. You are out of road. The* next *dangerous thing
> you fail, you spin the Death Roulette: roll one d6 against the number of
> Lethal Bullets on your sheet. You all start on one. Roll higher than it and
> you survive — and you tell us how you cheated death, and you add a bullet.
> Roll equal or lower and you are Left for Dead — unless another player spends
> their Spotlight to pull you out, and then they tell us how. Bullets never come
> off. So: you have to fail twelve boxes' worth, then fail again, then lose a
> coin-flip, and then nobody at this table spends a Spotlight on you. If all of
> that happens, it will have taken about three hours and you will have earned
> it."*

All of the above is verified in the transcription (Dangers notes, pp. 88–89,
102–103): 12 boxes, the 1/3/9/all ladder, Damage Control, *"With all Grit lost,
your next failed Dangerous Roll forces a spin on the Death Roulette"*, the
`> Lethal Bullets → survive, add 1` / `≤ → Left for Dead unless a friend spends
a Spotlight` rule, 1 starting bullet, and bullets never being removed.

## Overview

- **Event:** Contingency 2027 — **Sunday 31 January, Slot 14, afternoon**,
  Searles Leisure Resort, Hunstanton. **The convention's last game.**
- **System:** **Outgunned Adventure 1.1**
- **Setting:** **The Barranco de Sant Grau, Aragón, Spain — October 1938**
- **Tone:** Pulp register, real ground. Trucks, fists, a rope bridge and a
  dynamite budget, in a valley that has a name and a village that has a list.
- **Maximum Runtime:** 3 hours 30 minutes play + 10 minute break
- **Players:** 4–6. **Written for 5.** At four, **drop the pilot (Hale) and
  start the crew already in the valley.** Nothing essential lives on one PC.
- **Content warnings:** as in GM Essentials.
- **Art Style:** Ink sketch / pencil illustration. Direction: **dry limestone,
  October light, scaffolding.** **Nothing that comes out of the niche is ever
  depicted.** No uniforms or regalia as spectacle — field kit and clipboards.

### Design intent (read this first)

1. **The villains are legally and morally in the right, and that is the game.**
   Every instinct an adventure table has — *they're looting it, get it back* —
   is exactly inverted.
2. **The 1811 theft was the rescue.** The whole scenario is the players
   assembling that one sentence out of a provenance file, then having to act on
   it with twenty minutes and no proof anyone would accept.
3. **Pulp register, real ground.** The valley has a name, the village has
   names, and the people walking north are not scenery.
4. **No wonder weapon.** The reliquary does nothing for whoever holds it. It has
   one function, it performs it in one place, and that is why they want it in
   that place.
5. **The climax is a hole in the ground and a decision about a document.**

### Handling the material — a design constraint, not a disclaimer

Inherited from the DEAD AIR trilogy bible and adapted for a register where the
villains can be punched. **These five are binding at the table:**

1. **No wonder-weapon glamour.** The Ahnenerbe here are competent, well-funded
   and wrong. Nothing they have works better than it should.
2. **The Spanish dead are named.** Sant Grau has a list, and the list is `H4`.
3. **No uniforms or regalia as spectacle.** Field kit and clipboards. The menace
   is the authority behind it, not the tailoring.
4. **The war is not a backdrop for a treasure hunt.** The refugee column is in
   **two scenes** and is not used for atmosphere.
5. **Nobody is secretly a good German.** Ritter believes he is doing scholarship
   and restitution. He is sincere. **That is worse.**

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**The niche in the rock face above the Barranco de Sant Grau is not a shrine.
It is a fitting.** The reliquary sits in it the way a key sits in a lock, and
what it opens is not a door.

For six hundred years the village understood the arrangement well enough to
keep the thing in it and leave it alone.

In **1811** a French quartermaster named **Hilaire Fournel** came through with
Suchet's army, was told what the niche was by a priest who had no better
options, and **removed the reliquary deliberately** — writing it up as a
requisition because there was no form for what he was actually doing. He sent
it north through four sets of hands and it surfaced at a Vienna auction house
in 1874.

**Dr Albrecht Ritter**, 51, Ahnenerbe, bought it for the Reich in 1932 on the
strength of a genuinely excellent piece of scholarship: he reconstructed the
1811 route, matched the reliquary to the niche, and proved the object's origin
beyond reasonable doubt. **He is about to open the lock, because he believes the
niche is a reliquary station and the restoration is an act of repair.**

**What comes out** is the scenario's one piece of restraint. It is **not
described until Act Three, not statted, not fought, and does not negotiate.**
The scenario's threat is **the opening**, not the thing — and the thing will go
north with the retreating army, which is the ending nobody wants.

**The village of Sant Grau** has known since 1811 and has said nothing, because
saying it brought the French. **Forty-one people remain.** **Rosalía Benach**,
74, is the one who will talk, and **she wants the same thing Ritter wants — the
thing back in the niche — for the exact opposite reason.** She is the hardest
NPC in the scenario to argue with, and **Nuria Benach is her granddaughter**, so
the hardest argument in the game is inside one family.

### Revelations (GM checklist)

1. A German survey column has spent nine days in a dry riverbed sixty
   kilometres behind the line, doing nothing anyone can see.
2. They are not digging anything up. **They brought something with them.**
3. The crate holds a twelfth-century reliquary.
4. **They have an Austrian export licence, a bill of sale, and a provenance
   file three centimetres thick. All of it is genuine.**
5. The file proves the object was taken from this valley in 1811.
6. **Ritter's scholarship is excellent and his facts are all correct.**
7. The restoration is scheduled for **Thursday at first light**.
8. The niche is not a shrine. **It is a fitting.**
9. **Fournel's 1811 note, on the reverse: "removed for safety, by order, and
   not to be returned."** He was not stealing it.
10. **The village has known since 1811** and said nothing, because saying it
    brought the French.
11. **Rosalía wants it back in the niche too** — for the opposite reason — and
    will not explain herself twice.
12. **The reliquary does nothing for whoever holds it.** There is no prize.

**Undelivered by 2:45?** Rosalía says 8, 9, 10 and 11 in one speech. **Act
Three has no new information in it.**

## Player Experience

You are in Spain for six different reasons and none of them was this. A priest
with no parish left shows you a photograph of a German lorry parked in a dry
riverbed and asks, politely, whether you would mind.

By the second hour you have the survey column's order of march, a rope bridge
you should not have crossed, and a file of documents that proves beyond
argument that the Germans are the rightful owners of the thing in the crate.

By the third hour you have read the 1811 receipt properly — the one signed by a
French quartermaster who did not list the reliquary as plunder, but as
*removed for safety, by order, and not to be returned* — and worked out that he
was not stealing it.

By Thursday morning you are standing over a niche cut into rock, holding a
crate nobody can safely own, with a column of lorries coming up the barranco.

**Shape:** the north road; the barranco seen from above; the scaffolding, the
crate and the order of march; then first contact with the column — **papers,
politeness and a clipboard** — which is the act's whole point. The table
arrives braced for a fight and is handed an export licence.

### The two refugee scenes — constraint 4

**This is the first of the two.** The north road is full of people walking.
**Play it once, properly, for about four minutes, and do not return to it for
atmosphere.** Nobody in the column is a plot hook; there is no child to rescue.
A woman asks the PCs whether the road ahead is open, and they either know or
they do not. Then it is behind them. *(The second is in the Epilogue.)*

## ACT TWO — *The File* *(~90 minutes)*

**Shape: two tracks and a Run.** Track A is Sant Grau and Rosalía. Track B is
the column's camp and the file itself. **The truck chase sits between them.**
Split the table, swap at about the halfway mark, and bring them together for
the chase.

### Track A — Sant Grau

Forty-one people. A square, a dry fountain, and a church that has not been used
for anything since July.

**Rosalía Benach, 74**, is sitting outside. She knows exactly who the Germans
are and what Thursday is, and **she wants them to succeed.**

> *"My grandmother was told by the priest who told the Frenchman. It is a lock,
> and the thing they are bringing is the key, and it has been standing open
> since 1811. You have seen the valley. Do you think this is what open looks
> like, or do you think this is a bad year?"*
>
> *"I will not say it twice. I have said it once in my life and that was to
> you."*

**She will not explain herself twice** — and she does not argue. She states it
and goes back to her beans. **The table has to decide whether a 74-year-old
woman with no proof is the most reliable witness in the scenario**, which she
is.

**Nuria's seam:** Nuria Benach is her granddaughter. If Nuria is at the table,
Rosalía talks to her and not to the party, and the argument happens inside one
family in front of everybody. **This is the best scene in Act Two. Give it
room.**

### Track B — The camp and the file

The provenance file is **three centimetres thick and entirely genuine**: bill of
sale, 1932 export licence, 1874 auction catalogue entry, and underneath all of
it the **1811 requisition** (`H2`).

**Ritter will let them read it.** Getting it out of the camp is harder, but
reading it at his field desk with coffee is free, and that is where the
scenario turns.

### THE REVERSE — three routes and a fallback

*The outline leans "earn it". **Settled: earn it, with three routes and a
written fallback**, because it is the hinge of the whole scenario and the
scenario must not be able to stall on it.*

**What it is:** the 1811 requisition, front, is a quartermaster's inventory
line. **On the reverse, in Fournel's own hand:**

> *removed for safety, by order, and not to be returned*

**Route 1 — the right approach, automatic.** Anyone who physically turns the
document over. **Say "you turn it over" the instant a player touches it**; do
not make them ask. Alícia's ARCHEOLOGY and any Focus+Know approach to the
*document as an object* rather than its contents gets there free.

**Route 2 — written fallback.** `[OG: Focus+Know, Basic]` to read Napoleonic
quartermaster's French, or `[OG: Smooth+Speech, Basic]` to get Ritter to
translate it — **and he will, and he will not understand what he has just
said.** He reads it as a formality. *"By order. Yes — he had a requisition
number, you see, it is all above board."* **That is the scene.** He translates
his own refutation aloud and files it.

**Route 3 — the Countdown.** If it has not landed by **2:25**, Rosalía delivers
it: she has never seen the document and does not need to. *"He wrote that he
was taking it away for safety. I know because it is the only reason anyone
would."*

**Failure case:** if the table reaches 2:45 without the reverse, **run Act Three
anyway.** Rosalía is in Act Three and will say it over the crate. The decision
is still available; it is only less earned.

### THE SET-PIECE: THE LORRY, UP THE BARRANCO — GM quick-reference

*Somebody takes a vehicle, or the crate, or both, and the column comes after
them down a dry riverbed with no room to turn.*

**Rules note — read before running it.** Outgunned **Adventure 1.1 does not
print chase rules.** It prints **Runs** (pp. 164–169) and says in its own first
line: *"A Run is not a chase. It is a desperate race for survival against a
deadly trap or an overwhelming mob of Enemies."* The sidebar adds that **Chase
Special Actions from other Outgunned books are compatible** (1-Adrenaline
actions on Turn 1, 2-Adrenaline on Turn 2, and so on).

**So this is built as a Run, deliberately, and that is a house adaptation
rather than a printed chase.** It is the right chassis: a riverbed with three
lorries in it and no room to turn is an overwhelming mob in a corridor. **If you
own Outgunned core and prefer its Chase, it drops in** — the sheets support
either.

**THE RUN — 3 Turns, Critical Reaction.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **Duration** | **3 Turns.** Mark three boxes; fill one per Reaction Turn. **Do not tell the players it is three** (p. 166: the Director need not reveal it) |
| **Every roll is a Dangerous Roll** | Failure fills Grit by difficulty — **Critical = 3 boxes** — with **Damage Control** as normal |
| **Reaction difficulty** | **Critical** on Turns 1 and 2, **2 Critical** on Turn 3 |
| **Free Reactions** | **Yes — let players pick the Attribute+Skill that fits** (p. 166 step 3). This is how Alícia and Serafín stay in a car chase |
| **Winning** | Survive three Turns, **or end it early** by stopping the source — blocking the riverbed, dropping the scaffold, reaching Sant Grau |
| **Losing** | Only if **all** participating Adventurers lose all Grit. They survive: taken, and the crate goes back in the lorry |

**From Turn 2 onward:**
- **Fall Behind** — anyone who fails to score even a Basic takes a **Hindrance**
  on their next roll, and looks Broken if nobody helps. **An ally who slows to
  help: both take the Hindrance, neither is Broken.**
- **Find an Opening** — anyone who loses **no Grit at all** gets one **Action
  Roll** of their choice afterwards: end the Run early, Help a friend, or make
  the next Turn easier.

**Special Actions — use at most 3, one per Turn (p. 168). Recommended here:**

| Turn | Action | In this riverbed |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **By Surprise** | Degen's lorry is already across the mouth. No Feats or Luck on the next Reaction |
| 2 | **Detour** | The riverbed is blocked. **Duration +1** — you are now in a 4-Turn Run and they do not know it |
| 3 | **Dead End** | One Action Turn to hide or stop the danger, **or lose the Run** |

**Reaction prompts, if you want them in hand:**
1. the bed narrows between two boulders — `[OG: Nerves+Drive]`
2. a wheel goes into soft gravel — `[OG: Brawn+Force]`
3. the tarpaulin comes loose and the crate shifts — `[OG: Brawn+Stunt]`
4. a lorry comes abreast on the inside — `[OG: Nerves+Cool]`
5. the rope bridge, and the question of weight — `[OG: Focus+Fix]`
6. somebody is shooting, badly, from a moving vehicle — `[OG: Crime+Awareness]`

**If it becomes a straight firefight with the column:** it can, it is Outgunned,
and it is survivable. **Treat the column as a single Enemy** (Adventure p. 105:
*"Adventurers always face a single Enemy per combat"*) — **Grit 8, Attack 2
Basic, Defense Critical.** Degen's men are trained but they are a survey escort
and they do not want to die in a riverbed in October. **Ritter is killable** —
see below.

### Is Ritter killable?

**Yes. Settled.** He is a 51-year-old scholar in a dry riverbed and the party
has four people who can shoot, drive or hit someone.

**And the restoration still happens on Thursday, at first light, under Degen** —
who does not care what is in the crate, has a schedule, and wants to be out of
Spain by November. **That is the point.** Killing Ritter removes the only person
in the valley who could be argued with and changes nothing about the deadline.

**Say this out loud through Degen** at the first opportunity after it happens,
so the table understands what they have done:

> *"The doctor had a schedule. I have the schedule. Thursday, first light. I do
> not know what it is for and I do not need to."*

## ACT THREE — *The Niche* *(~35 minutes)*

**First light, Thursday. No new information.**

Five people, a crate, a hole in a rock face, and a column of lorries coming up
the barranco.

> The scaffold is still up and the light is coming over the eastern rim in a
> flat grey sheet. The niche is cut into the limestone at the height of a man's
> chest, square, shallow, and tooled — somebody made it to hold one thing — and
> it has been empty for a hundred and twenty-seven years. Down the valley you
> can hear three engines in low gear.

### The decision

**Everyone gets to act.** The pregens are built so that the climax rolls are
**Know, Speech, Leadership, Detect, Awareness, Force and Cool** — not Fight and
Shoot. Six sheets, six defensible answers to *who does this belong to*, and the
decision has to be made out loud by a named player.

**Rosalía is there.** She walked up in the dark. She is not going to stop
anybody and she is not going to help.

### What comes out — **settled: by effect only, never statted, never fought**

*Open question 1, resolved. The reliquary is never fought and the scenario ends
before the thing is resolved.*

**If the niche is opened**, describe **three effects and nothing else:**

1. **The engines stop.** All three, at once, and so does everything that was
   making noise.
2. **The light goes wrong before anything moves** — the flat grey sheet over the
   eastern rim is still there and is no longer coming from the east.
3. **Everybody in the barranco turns to look up the valley at the same moment**,
   including the Germans, including Rosalía, including anyone who had decided
   not to.

**Then cut.** Do not describe a shape. Do not roll for it. **It does not
negotiate and it is not an Enemy** — there is no entry for it in any sheet, and
that is deliberate (see the audit in the pregens file: no PC carries a
supernatural edge and no sheet is built to fight it).

**If a player asks what it is, the honest answer is the GM's:** *"Nobody at this
table finds that out. The scenario is about whether the lock gets turned."*

### The four endings — all written, all played straight

| Ending | What it takes | What it reads like |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Buried elsewhere** | Take the crate out of the valley and put it somewhere else | **It is now your problem, permanently.** The niche stays open and empty, and somebody will come looking — Ritter's file is in a Berlin archive with a map in it |
| **2. Destroyed** | Try it. **It can be destroyed** | The scenario does not say what that costs until somebody tries, **and then it says so plainly: it takes everything in the valley that could have been used to close the niche instead.** The lock cannot be opened again and cannot be shut |
| **3. Restored** | Let Ritter — or Degen — put it back, with the correct ceremony | **Played straight and it is a real ending.** The lock is shut for the first time since 1811. **The epilogue is the village, not a monster** |
| **4. Taken north** | Somebody decides the thing is worth more than the valley | **Also written straight.** It goes north with the retreating army and so does the file. No judgement in the narration |

**Ending 3 is the one the table will resist hardest and it is the one Rosalía
asks for.** Do not steer. Let them be wrong on their own terms.

### If the column arrives mid-argument

It will — that is what the engines are for. **Degen does not want a firefight at
first light on a schedule.** He wants twenty minutes and the crate. **Give the
table a real chance to talk to him**, and remember that he genuinely does not
know or care what the niche is.

### Pacing Note — Act Three

**Must be true before the Epilogue:** the decision made out loud, by a named
player, with the others' positions stated.

**If behind:** skip the lorries' approach. Open on the niche, Rosalía already
there, and the engines already audible.

## Epilogue — *The List* *(~15 minutes)*

**Per-PC vignettes keyed to the ending.** Go round once; each player says where
their character is in November.

### Outcomes

| Outcome | What it reads like |
|---|---|
| **Buried elsewhere** | Four of you know where it is. By 1942 two of you are dead and one of those two told somebody |
| **Destroyed** | The barranco is quiet and the niche is open and will stay open, and the thing that was supposed to close it is gone. Nobody will know for a generation whether that mattered |
| **Restored** | The scaffold comes down on the Friday. The column is out of Spain by November, as Degen wanted. **Sant Grau is still there in 1975** |
| **Taken north** | It crosses the Pyrenees in a staff car in February 1939, with the file, and is catalogued in Berlin in April. **Everything after that is somebody else's scenario** |

### The second refugee scene — and the last line

**Constraint 4's other half.** On the way out, the north road is still full of
people walking, and it is **not** the same people — these have come further.
**Four lines, no dialogue, no hook.**

Then read the list. **`H4` — Sant Grau's forty-one names.**

> Forty-one people lived in Sant Grau in October 1938. Here is the list.

**Read it. All of it. That is the last thing that happens in the game, and it is
the last thing that happens at Contingency 2027.**

## The Tangent

*For a table that finishes early.*

**The four sets of hands.** Ritter's file reconstructs the 1811 route in detail:
Fournel sent the reliquary north through four intermediaries before it reached
Vienna in 1874.

**The third of them was a Spanish priest**, and the file notes without comment
that he held it for eleven years and that his parish — not Sant Grau, two
valleys west — has no record of him after 1829.

He was doing what Fournel did, for the same reason, and he kept it as long as he
could. **Nobody in this scenario is the first person to have had this problem,
and nobody has solved it yet.**

## Open questions — **resolved for v1**

1. **What comes out of the niche, and how much is shown?** → **Three effects
   and nothing else** (engines stop, the light comes from the wrong place,
   everybody turns at once), then cut. **Never statted, never fought, never
   depicted.** The GM's honest answer to *what is it* is written into Act Three.
2. **Is Ritter killable in Act Two?** → **Yes**, and **the restoration happens
   on Thursday under Degen regardless**, which is the point. Degen's line is
   scripted.
3. **Does the table get the 1811 reverse, or earn it?** → **Earn it**, with
   three routes (turning it over, reading the French or getting **Ritter** to
   translate his own refutation aloud, and the Countdown at **2:25** through
   Rosalía) and a written failure case that keeps Act Three reachable.
4. **How much Civil War does a four-hour pulp game carry?** → **Two scenes,
   bounded and written**: the north road in Act One (about four minutes) and
   the second column in the Epilogue (four lines). **The list is read in full.**
   Flagged to watch at the first playtest, as the outline asks.
5. **The Death Roulette needs explaining in under ninety seconds.** →
   **Scripted verbatim in GM Essentials**, with every rule in it checked
   against the Adventure 1.1 dangers text.
6. **Outgunned core or Outgunned Adventure?** *(raised by the pregens)* →
   **Adventure 1.1.** All six sheets are built to it; the Run replaces the core
   book's Chase; the citations are to Adventure.

### New open questions, raised by writing v1

1. **The match ladder is the one unverified rule in this document.** The four
   difficulty names and the whole Grit/Death Roulette apparatus are cited; the
   *two-of-a-kind = Basic* mapping is from memory. **Check one page before the
   first playtest** and delete the `[unverified]` note.
2. **The Run is a house adaptation and says so.** Adventure 1.1 states that a
   Run is not a chase. The chassis fits a riverbed with no room to turn, but a
   playtest should confirm it feels like a chase and not like a trap sequence.
   If it does not, Outgunned core's Chase drops in.
3. **Ending 3 is the correct ending and the table will hate it.** The scenario
   deliberately does not steer. If playtests show tables reliably refuse it out
   of reflex rather than argument, the fix is to give Rosalía one more scene in
   Act Two, not to make Ritter more sympathetic.
4. **Six positions is a lot of argument for a last-slot table.** The pregens
   are built as a six-way disagreement. At six players on a Sunday afternoon at
   the end of a convention, that may be one or two positions too many — watch
   whether the table reaches a decision by 3:20.

## GM Notes (private)

- **Ritter is the hardest thing in this scenario to play and the whole game
  depends on it.** He is not smug, not a fanatic, and not secretly cruel. He is
  a careful man who did six years of excellent work and reached a conclusion
  that is correct in every particular and catastrophic. **Let him be likeable.**
  That is constraint 5 doing its job.
- **Never argue the table's case for them.** Do not have an NPC point out that
  the 1811 note changes everything. Rosalía states a belief; Ritter states
  facts; the connection is the players' to make.
- **There is no prize.** The reliquary does nothing for whoever holds it. If the
  table starts treating it as loot, say so plainly through Alícia's or
  Baltasar's expertise.
- **Degen is the pressure and Ritter is the argument.** Keep them apart in Act
  Two if you can — the scenario gets worse when the man with the schedule and
  the man with the case are in the same scene.
- **The list is not a gut-punch to be timed.** Read it flatly. Forty-one names,
  no music, no pause for effect, and then the session is over.
