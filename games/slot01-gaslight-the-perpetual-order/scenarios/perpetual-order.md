### Design intent (read this first)

1. **The smallest game on the slate.** One location, four people, a padlock, a
   corridor and a hundred and two crates. The whole scenario fits on a table.
2. **Closing energy.** Sunday afternoon, half the hall already packing. This game
   wants to be quiet, finite, strange and finished — not a sprint, and not a
   gut-punch.
3. **No antagonist at all.** Nobody to fight, nobody to arrest, nobody to blame.
   There is an arrangement, and it has a vacancy.
4. **The horror is clerical.** A banker's order, an inventory, a reference number
   and a date. Everything frightening here is a piece of paperwork that does not
   add up, and the player characters are exactly the sort of people who notice
   paperwork.
5. **End on the image.** Crate 102 exists, it is empty, and the label is in this
   year's date. Get the table to that and stop.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**Peter Shearing**, a 34-year-old counting-house clerk, walked into the river at
Wapping Stairs on the night of **Thursday 12 June 1794**, and did not drown, and
did not quite come out either. What he found — or what found him; the scenario
never says — is not a creature. It is closer to a **mooring**: a place where a
person can be kept from drifting, provided the mooring is paid for and provided
something is tied to it.

Shearing has been living Friday 13 June 1794 ever since. Not a loop, which is the
mistake the players will make first: it is a **drift**. Every year he reaches that
Friday again and every year it has moved a little further from the one that
actually happened. He knows, because he is a clerk, and so every year he crates up
one day's papers and files them in order, as evidence of how far he has gone. He
drew the banker's order on the Thursday, out of the account he had, and has
renewed it by post a hundred times.

**The drift, readable from the crates in order:**
- Crates 1–20 are indistinguishable from a real Friday in 1794.
- Around crate 40 the newspaper's minor items have changed; the shipping lists
  have not.
- By crate 70 there are two chandlers on the receipted bills that have never
  traded in Wapping, and the coin is right but the King's head faces the other way.
- Crate 95 holds a coach ticket for a stage to a place name that is almost a place.
- **Crate 101** — last June — holds one receipted bill, from Garrow's Bonded
  Wharf, for the quarter's rent on vault 114, dated 13 June 1794, in a currency
  that is sterling and is not.

**Crate 102 is already in the vault.** Knocked together, empty, with its label
written in the same hand as all the others and dated **this June** — which has not
happened yet.

**The vacancy.** The mooring is paid, and it holds, and it does not care who is
tied to it. Shearing has been at the far end of a rope for a hundred and one years
and is very nearly out of reach. The moment the order lapses — which the bank's
clerk among the player characters has the authority to stop today, with a
signature — the rope goes slack.

**What the players can actually do:**
- **Let it lapse.** Shearing comes loose. The epilogue is four ordinary people
  going home, and a crate 102 that fills itself anyway, with their papers.
- **Keep paying.** The order can be assigned. It needs a name, a signature and an
  account, and it is a commitment with no end and no way to explain it to a wife.
  It also, demonstrably, works.
- **Reach along the rope.** Shearing can be answered: the direction for
  correspondence on the 1794 order is a receiving-house in Shadwell that still
  takes letters, and letters left there are answered by the next morning. Writing
  to him is the best scene in the scenario and gives the table the terms. He will
  not ask to be let go. He has been very careful never to ask anyone for anything.
- **Clear the vault** as instructed and walk away. The job is done, the paperwork
  is clean, crate 102 is on the barge, and it is still filling.

## Player Experience

Vault 114 has been paid for by a banker's order every quarter since June 1794.
Garrow's Bonded Wharf was built in 1829. Before that the order paid a bonded
store in Rotherhithe that burned, and before that a cellar under a chandler's
that was pulled down, and before that there is a ledger reference and no building
at all.

Nobody has ever opened it. The dock company wants the vault cleared because the
wharf comes down in March, and the bank wants the account closed because it has
been flagged dormant for sixty years despite being, demonstrably, not dormant.
Between the four of you, you have the authority to strike the padlock. It takes
nine seconds.

Inside are a hundred and one identical deal crates in four neat stacks, numbered
1 to 101, and each one holds a single ordinary day's leavings. A newspaper. Two
receipted bills. A playbill. A coach ticket. A tide-table.

They are all from Friday the thirteenth of June 1794.

They are not all from the same Friday the thirteenth of June 1794.

## Open questions (carried from the outline — resolve or delete)

1. Three inventory handouts or ten? Ten is better horror and a real print cost.
   Current lean: three printed, the rest described, with the GM holding a full
   drift table.
2. Does Shearing's reply get a written script, or improv notes? A script is safer
   and risks flatness; this is the scene the whole scenario is built to reach.
3. Wapping, or a provincial port? London is richer and the fog is free. A Lynn or
   Yarmouth wharf would be a nice touch an hour from the convention and loses the
   river.
4. Is "a gentle horror with no antagonist" enough for 3h30, or does Act Two need
   one hard beat to keep a tired table awake? Current intent: one, and it is a
   sound from the corridor that turns out to be the wharfinger's own dog — a
   deliberate false alarm, and the only one.
