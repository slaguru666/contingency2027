## GM ESSENTIALS (one glance, any GM)

- **System crash-course:** Call of Cthulhu 7e, Gaslight. Roll d100 against a
  skill. Equal or under = success; under half = hard success; under a fifth =
  extreme. 01–05 is a critical, 96–00 a fumble (100 always fumbles if the skill
  is under 50). Push a failed roll by stating what the investigator does *more*
  of — on a second failure the Keeper imposes a real consequence. Sanity: lose
  the stated points, then roll d100 against current Sanity; **over** it means a
  temporary bout of madness.
- **Mechanics inline:** `[CoC: Skill — what it reveals or does]`
- **There is no monster, no antagonist and no combat.** If you find yourself
  reaching for a threat, reach for a piece of paper instead.
- **The clock counts play time** — pause it for the break.
- **Hard rule: at 2:45, go to Act Three regardless.** Act Two will happily eat
  the whole session; it must not.
- **Sanity on offer: 3 points at best, 12 at worst** across eight crate
  bands (four at 0/1, two at 1/1D3, one at 1/1D2) — so a thorough table
  typically takes 6 or 7. This is a quiet game. Do not add losses.
- **Safety:** state the tool in use (X-card / open door) during seating — 30
  seconds, no ceremony. The identity-loss material in Act Three wants the exit
  ramp genuinely available.
- **The one permitted jolt** is the sound in the corridor in Act Two, and it is
  the wharfinger's own dog. There is not a second one. Resist.

## Overview

- **Event:** Contingency 2027 — Wednesday 27 January, Slot 1, morning,
  Searles Leisure Resort, Hunstanton. **The convention's opening game.**
- **System:** Call of Cthulhu 7e — Cthulhu by Gaslight, **February 1895**
- **Setting:** **Garrow's Bonded Wharf**, Wapping. A counting-house, a cold
  brick corridor, and vault 114. One site, one afternoon, in the fog.
- **Tone:** Clerical, patient, cold. A ledger that does not add up, read by
  people whose whole profession is making ledgers add up.
- **Maximum Runtime:** 3 hours 30 minutes play + 10 minute break
- **Players:** 4–6. Six sheets exist; the game runs at four. See the roster for
  the drop order — the newspaperman goes first and the lighterman second, and
  their lanes (outside knowledge, river knowledge) are recoverable from the
  wharfinger and the searcher respectively.
- **Content warnings:** claustrophobia and enclosed spaces; loss of identity
  and the fear of it; a drowning and an implied suicide in the backstory;
  existential dread played quietly. **No gore, no violence, no jump scares.**
  Safety tools in use.
- **Art Style:** Ink sketch / pencil illustration. Scenario direction: dock
  furniture and paperwork, never faces. Crates, hasps, tide-marks, a lamp, the
  grain of deal boards. Shearing is never depicted.

### Design intent (read this first)

1. **The smallest game on the slate.** One location, one afternoon, a padlock,
   a corridor and a hundred and two crates. The whole scenario fits on a table.
2. **It was written as the convention's closer and now opens it.** That is a
   real change and this draft answers it rather than hiding it. A 9am Wednesday
   table is *fresh, not tired* — it has not been playing all week, it has been
   in a car. So the quiet is now a courtesy rather than a wind-down: nobody has
   to perform energy they do not have at nine in the morning. What was cut is
   the valedictory note in Act Three. The game no longer ends like the end of
   something; it ends like **a door closing behind four people who have to
   spend the next four days in the same hotel as each other.**
3. **No antagonist at all.** Nobody to fight, nobody to arrest, nobody to
   blame. There is an arrangement, and it has a vacancy.
4. **The horror is clerical.** A banker's order, an inventory, a reference
   number and a date. Everything frightening here is a piece of paperwork that
   does not add up, and the player characters are exactly the sort of people
   who notice paperwork.
5. **End on the image.** Crate 102 exists, it is empty, and the label is in
   this year's date. Get the table to that and stop.

**Deviation from the house format, stated:** there is **no set-piece**. The
house standard asks for one per scenario; this scenario's equivalent is Act
Two's crate-sorting, which is a table activity rather than a chase or a
séance, and it has its own quick-reference (the Drift Table). This is
deliberate and is the reason the game is cheap to produce.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**Peter Shearing**, a 34-year-old counting-house clerk, walked into the river
at Wapping Stairs on the night of **Thursday 12 June 1794**, and did not drown,
and did not quite come out either.

What he found — or what found him; the scenario never says, and must not — is
not a creature. It is closer to a **mooring**: a place where a person can be
kept from drifting, provided the mooring is paid for and provided something is
tied to it.

Shearing has been living Friday 13 June 1794 ever since. **Not a loop** — that
is the mistake the players will make first, and it is the right mistake to
make. It is a **drift**. Every year he reaches that Friday again and every year
it has moved a little further from the one that actually happened. He knows,
because he is a clerk. So every year he crates up one day's papers and files
them in order, as evidence of how far he has gone. He drew the banker's order
on the Thursday, out of the account he had, and has renewed it by post a
hundred times.

**The drift, readable from the crates in order:**

- Crates 1–20 are indistinguishable from a real Friday in 1794.
- Around crate 40 the newspaper's minor items have changed; the shipping lists
  have not.
- By crate 70 there are two chandlers on the receipted bills who have never
  traded in Wapping, and the coin is right but the King's head faces the other
  way.
- Crate 95 holds a coach ticket for a stage to a place name that is *almost* a
  place.
- **Crate 101** — last June — holds one receipted bill, from Garrow's Bonded
  Wharf, for the quarter's rent on vault 114, dated 13 June 1794, in a currency
  that is sterling and is not.

**Crate 102 is already in the vault.** Knocked together, empty, its label
written in the same hand as all the others and dated **this June** — which has
not happened yet.

**The vacancy.** The mooring is paid, and it holds, and it does not care who is
tied to it. Shearing has been at the far end of a rope for a hundred and one
years and is very nearly out of reach. The moment the order lapses — which the
bank's clerk among the player characters has the authority to stop today, with
a signature — the rope goes slack.

**What the players can actually do** (all four are written; none is correct):

- **Let it lapse.** Shearing comes loose. The epilogue is four ordinary people
  going home, and a crate 102 that fills itself anyway, with their papers.
- **Keep paying.** The order can be assigned. It needs a name, a signature and
  an account, and it is a commitment with no end and no way to explain it to a
  wife. It also, demonstrably, works.
- **Reach along the rope.** Shearing can be answered: the direction for
  correspondence on the 1794 order is a receiving-house in Shadwell that still
  takes letters, and letters left there are answered by the next morning.
  Writing to him is the best scene in the scenario and gives the table the
  terms. **He will not ask to be let go.** He has been very careful never to
  ask anyone for anything.
- **Clear the vault** as instructed and walk away. The job is done, the
  paperwork is clean, crate 102 is on the barge, and it is still filling.

### Revelations (GM checklist — tick them off; see Act Two's Pacing Note)

1. Vault 114's rent has been paid every quarter since June 1794.
2. The payer has never once been seen, and the renewals come by post.
3. The order has outlived three buildings and kept the same vault number.
4. The bank thinks the account is dormant; the account is demonstrably not.
5. The vault has never been opened. Nobody can produce a reason why not.
6. The crates each hold one ordinary day's paper leavings.
7. Every crate is the same date: Friday 13 June 1794.
8. **They are not the same day.** The contents disagree with each other.
9. The disagreements run in one direction and get worse with the number — it is
   a drift, not a loop.
10. A man's name is on the 1794 order: Peter Shearing, clerk, of Wapping.
11. Shearing drowned — or did not — on 12 June 1794. A parish entry says one
    thing and a coroner's note another.
12. Letters left at the Shadwell receiving-house are answered by morning.
13. Crate 102 exists, is empty, and is labelled with a date that has not come.

**Anything not delivered by the end of Act Two folds into Act Three's letter
scene**, which can carry 10, 11 and 12 on its own. Beyond that, Act Three
contains **no new information** — only confirmation and consequence.

## Player Experience

Vault 114 has been paid for by a banker's order every quarter since June 1794.
Garrow's Bonded Wharf was built in 1829. Before that the order paid a bonded
store in Rotherhithe that burned, and before that a cellar under a chandler's
that was pulled down, and before that there is a ledger reference and no
building at all.

Nobody has ever opened it. The dock company wants the vault cleared because the
wharf comes down in March, and the bank wants the account closed because it has
been flagged dormant for sixty years despite being, demonstrably, not dormant.
Between you, you have the authority to strike the padlock. It takes nine
seconds.

Inside are a hundred and one identical deal crates in four neat stacks,
numbered 1 to 101, and each one holds a single ordinary day's leavings. A
newspaper. Two receipted bills. A playbill. A coach ticket. A tide-table.

They are all from Friday the thirteenth of June 1794.

They are not all from the same Friday the thirteenth of June 1794.

## The Countdown (background clock)

Nothing here is hostile. The pressure is entirely administrative, and that is
the point: **the world is politely asking these people to stop looking.**
Advance it when the table circles.

| Clock | What happens | How it is felt |
|---|---|---|
| 0:40 | The fog thickens off the river. | The counting-house windows go from grey to white. The gas is lit at eleven in the morning. |
| 1:10 | The dock company's lighter is confirmed for **four o'clock** to take the vault's contents. | A boy brings a note. Six hours, then five, then four. |
| 1:50 | The lamp. There is no light in the vault but what they carry, and the wharf keeps **one** spare canister of oil. | "That's about four hours of burning, sirs, if you don't turn it up." |
| 2:20 | The agent's second letter arrives, having crossed the first. It asks whether the vault is cleared and reminds them Lady Day is Lady Day. | Read it aloud. It does not mention crates. It does not know there are crates. |
| 2:40 | The lighterman starts loading from the **far** stack — the high numbers — because that stack is nearest the door. | If the players have not read crate 101 yet, this is how they are made to. |
| 3:05 | Four o'clock. The lighter casts off, loaded or not. | The tide does not wait, and the tide-table in crate 3 is still correct. |

**If the table stalls at any point**, the lever is always the same shape: *a
person who is owed an answer asks for it.* Garrow wants to go home. The agent
wants a signature. The lighterman wants to know which stack. Nobody threatens
anybody in this scenario.

## Introduction — *Nine Seconds' Work* *(~15 minutes, including Slot Zero)*

### Slot Zero (before the fiction)

1. Seating, and the safety line: *"We have an X-card and the door is always
   open. This game is about paperwork and about not being sure you are still
   yourself. If either gets uncomfortable, say so or tap, and we move on. No
   explanation needed."* Thirty seconds.
2. Pitch each sheet in one spoken line — the essence line off the roster.
3. Players choose, or deal them. Five minutes, maximum.
4. **Then open in motion.** Nobody arrives; everybody is already cold.

### Opening (read aloud or paraphrase)

> The fog came up the river with the tide and has decided to stay. Garrow's
> Bonded Wharf stands in it like a thing remembered rather than seen — four
> floors of blackened brick, a crane arm, and a smell of tar, rope and old
> pepper. You have each been sent here this morning by somebody different, about
> the same vault, and none of you was told the others were coming.

### Player Briefing

Say this plainly, in your own words, before any rolls:

- The wharf comes down in March. Everything in it must be out by Lady Day.
- **Vault 114** has been rented continuously since 1794 by a banker's order
  that has never once failed, and has never once been visited.
- The bank wants the account closed. The dock company wants the vault emptied.
  The lease wants a witness. The demolition wants a valuation.
- **Between them, the people at this table have the authority to open it.** No
  warrant, no permission, no waiting. It is nine seconds' work with a chisel.
- Nobody at this table has to be talked into anything. They are all here *to do
  this job.* The question the game asks is what they do once it is done.

### Atmosphere Notes

- **Cold, not dark.** It is a working wharf at eleven in the morning. There are
  men shouting two floors up. The horror has an audience and does not care.
- **Everything is slightly damp.** Paper, wool, brick, the back of the neck.
- **No echoes in the corridor.** Say this once, early, as a pleasant fact about
  good brickwork. Let the table remember it later.

### Hook into Act One

Garrow has the wharf's day-book open before anyone sits down, and he wants
this over with. The first thing on the table is not the vault. It is a
question of whose paperwork is correct — and all of it is.

## ACT ONE — *The Paperwork* *(~60 minutes)*

**Shape:** a counting-house, four strangers, three sets of documents that each
say something true and incompatible. Unhurried on purpose. The pleasure is
clerical archaeology and the act ends with a cold chisel.

### Summary

The players establish that the order is impossibly old, that it has followed
the vault number through three buildings, and that nobody has ever looked
inside. Then they open it.

### Locations

**The counting-house** — a long deal table, a ledger press, a copying machine,
a coal stove that works, and a window that has gone white. Garrow's day-book,
the wharf's rent ledgers back to 1829, the bank's day-book brought in a
satchel, the dock company's instruction. *This is where Act One happens.*

**The corridor to 114** — brick, barrel-vaulted, forty feet, doors on the left
numbered 108 to 118. Cold enough to see your breath. Gas bracket at the near
end only; past that, carry a lamp. **No echo.**

**Vault 114** — eight feet by twelve, dry, brick, one door, no window. A hasp
and a staple and a padlock whose maker went out of business in 1851. Four neat
stacks of deal crates. *Do not describe the stacks in detail until Act Two.*

### NPCs

**Garrow, the wharfinger** *(a pregen — see the roster)*
*Essence: eleven years on this wharf, and has started this week to notice that
he always walks a little faster past 114.*
If nobody is playing him, he is present as an NPC and says so when asked: no,
he has never opened it; no, he has never been asked to; and no, he cannot tell
you why not, and he has started to find that interesting.

**The dock company's agent — by letter only.** *Wants the vault cleared by Lady
Day.* The committee reduced to correspondence. Two letters arrive during play
(see The Countdown). He is not a villain, he is a deadline, and he never
appears. **If a player insists on going to find him**, the office is in
Leadenhall Street, it is an hour each way in this fog, and he will say exactly
what his letters say. Let them go if they want; the scenario is better if they
do not, and Garrow will say so.

**Mr Pike, the lighterman.** *Wants to know which stack, and to catch his
tide.* Arrives at 2:40 on the clock. Phlegmatic, helpful, utterly incurious,
and the only person in the scenario with somewhere else to be. He is the
instrument of the Countdown's last turn. **He will carry crates out if asked**,
and he will not notice anything about them.

### Clue Trail — Act One

**Fallbacks, one per essential clue:**
- **1, 2:** if the players do not read ledgers, Garrow reads them aloud. He is
  proud of his day-book.
- **3:** the bank's clerk is *required by his instructions* to verify the
  signature on renewal, so he must look at the hand.
- **4:** if `Spot Hidden` fails, the agent's first letter (1:10) quotes the
  Shadwell direction back at them as the address they should have written to.
- **5:** the Countdown's fog beat gives Garrow something to do: he tells the
  story of the chandler's cellar unprompted, because his father worked it.
- **8:** Pike asks it, flatly, when he arrives: *"Nobody's had that off, then?"*

### The obvious professional moves — answer them, do not stonewall

Players at this table are *professionals*, and they will do competent things.
Each of these has a written result that moves the trail forward:

- **"I write to the bank for the account's opening papers."** A reply is
  possible by the afternoon post: the account was opened 12 June 1794 in the
  name of **Peter Shearing, clerk, of Wapping**, and has had no other signatory.
  This is Revelation 10, delivered early, and that is fine.
- **"I check the parish register for Shearing."** St John's, Wapping, ten
  minutes' walk. There is a burial entry for 14 June 1794 — *and* a coroner's
  marginal note that no body was recovered. Both are in the same ledger. This is
  Revelation 11 and it is better found here than in the letter.
- **"I go to the receiving-house now."** Shadwell is twenty-five minutes in
  fog. They will take a letter. They will not say who collects, because the
  arrangement is older than anyone working there and is paid annually.
- **"I value the contents for the demolition."** The surveyor's own job. A
  hundred and one crates of waste paper, worth eleven shillings as pulp. Write
  the figure down; it will be funny and then it will not be.
- **"I refuse to open it without written authority."** Garrow produces the
  clearance instruction. It is written authority. The scenario cannot be
  stonewalled from inside.

### Story Hooks (if players stall)

- Garrow's dog whines at the corridor door and will not go past 114. He kicks
  it, embarrassed, and apologises to the dog. **Plant this now** — it is the
  Act Two jolt's setup, and it must be planted in Act One or the jolt is a
  cheat.
- The coal stove goes out and the room is cold in four minutes.
- The bank's clerk's instructions include a time by which he must report.

### The padlock

When they decide to open it: no roll. It is a hasp and a staple and a
seventy-year-old lock. `[CoC: no roll — the chisel does it in nine seconds, and
say the nine seconds out loud]`. If a player wants to pick it instead, let
them, and it takes longer and achieves exactly the same.

> The hasp comes away with the noise of one nail giving up. The door opens
> inward on dry hinges. There is no rush of air, no smell of anything, and no
> sound at all — and the four of you are standing in the only light there is.

### Pacing Note — Act One

**Must be true before Act Two:** the order's impossible age is on the table;
at least two of the three buildings traced; the padlock struck.

**Fast-track if you are past 1:05:** skip clues 6 and 7, have Garrow narrate
the building history as anecdote while someone fetches the chisel, and go
straight to the door. **Nothing in Act One is worth Act Two's time.**

**Place the break here**, on the first crate opened and nothing happening.

## ACT TWO — *The Crates* *(~90 minutes)*

**Shape:** the scenario's whole body. A hundred and one crates, read in
whatever order the players choose — **and the order they choose is the game**,
because reading 1, 50, 101 tells a different story from reading 99, 100, 101.
Hand out the inventories and let them sort.

**The GM's job here is pacing and restraint.** No manifestations. No noises
except the one. Nothing behind anybody. Just paper.

### What they see first

> Four stacks, shoulder-high, of identical deal crates — the cheap kind, two
> boards and a batten, the sort a counting-house throws together for its own
> storage. Each has a number brushed on the end in black: a column of ones and
> twos and threes, rising. The air is dry. The brick is dry. A hundred and one
> crates in a room that has been shut for a hundred and one years, and there is
> no dust on any of them.

Say the no-dust line once and do not explain it. Nobody in the fiction can
explain it either.

### How reading a crate works

Opening and reading one crate properly takes **about four minutes of table
time**. The contents are always the same shape:

> A newspaper, folded. Two receipted bills. A playbill or a handbill. A ticket
> of some kind. A tide-table. Occasionally a personal scrap — a laundry list, a
> pawn ticket, a note about a dog.

**No roll is needed to read a crate.** `[CoC: no roll resolves this — the
contents are plain and the investigators are literate professionals.]` Rolls
buy *extra*:

- `[CoC: Accounting — the sums on the bills are right, and in which currency]`
- `[CoC: History — whether a named person, ship or playhouse actually existed]`
- `[CoC: Natural World — the tide-table is correct for that date, every time]`
- `[CoC: Art/Craft (Penmanship) — one hand, slowly tiring, across a century]`
- `[CoC: Spot Hidden — the crate numbers are in the same hand as the labels]`

### THE DRIFT TABLE — GM quick-reference

**This is the set-piece substitute. Keep it open.** Players will name crate
numbers; answer from the band. Each band gives you the newspaper, the bills,
the ticket and the **tell** — the one thing in that band that is wrong.

| Crates | The newspaper | The bills | The ticket | **The tell** | Sanity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1–20** | *The Times*, Fri 13 Jun 1794. Correct in every particular. | Two chandlers who really traded: Bowyer, and Sim & Nephew. | Coach ticket, Whitechapel to Romford. | **Nothing is wrong.** This is the real day. Let them enjoy being wrong about that. | — |
| **21–40** | Masthead and shipping lists correct. **Minor items changed** — a different horse wins at Epsom, a drowning reported on a different stair. | Same chandlers, pennies different. | Same stage, a different departure hour. | The *small* news has moved and the *large* news has not. **Sanity budget check.** A player who reads every band rolls against seven
entries: 0/1 four times, 1/1D3 twice, 1/1D2 once. **Best case 3, worst case
12, typical 6–7** — survivable by design, and the whole scenario's budget.
**Do not add losses.** If a
player does something brave or terrible, the cost is in Act Three, not here.

### Running 90 minutes of reading paper without it sagging

Break the act into three movements. Each has a checkpoint, and each ends on a
pull.

**Movement One — "They're all the same day" (~25 min).** Let them open four or
five crates from anywhere. The discovery is the date, repeated. Players will
say *loop*. Let them. Let them build the theory, and let one of them write it
down.
*Ends on:* somebody comparing two crates side by side and finding they
disagree.

**Movement Two — "It's not a loop" (~40 min).** The sort. This is the heart of
the scenario and the table should be physically moving paper around. Push them
toward sequence: *which way does it get worse?* The drift reads in one
direction, and establishing the direction is the actual puzzle.
*Ends on:* the coin from the 61–80 band on the table, facing the wrong way.
*Ask whose coin it is.*

**Movement Three — "Somebody is doing this" (~25 min).** Shift from the paper
to the filer. One hand, a century, no dust, the tide always right. The
penmanship roll. The Shadwell direction remembered from Act One. The name
Shearing, if it has not already landed.
*Ends on:* crate 101 and `H2-C`.

### The one permitted jolt

At roughly **1:50**, once and only once:

> Something moves in the corridor. Not footsteps — a dragging, and a click, and
> a breath.

It is Garrow's dog, which has followed them down and will not come past the
door of 114. **Resolve it within thirty seconds.** It is a deliberate false
alarm and the only one in the game, and its purpose is to prove to the table
that this scenario does not do that — so that when nothing happens for the next
hour, the nothing is credible.

**If you did not plant the dog in Act One, do not use this beat.** An unplanted
jolt is a cheat. Use instead: the lamp guttering as the oil canister runs down,
which is on the Countdown anyway.

### Pacing Note — Act Two

**Must be true before Act Three:** the drift is understood as a *direction*,
not a repetition; crate 101 has been read; the Shadwell direction is known.

**Cuts, in the order to take them:**
1. Drop bands 41–60 and 81–94 entirely. Six bands tell the story as well as
   eight; nobody is counting.
2. Drop Revelations 14 and 16 (tide-tables, the reappearing name).
3. Collapse Movement Three into Act Three's letter — the letter carries
   Shearing's identity on its own.

**At 2:45 you go to Act Three whether or not any of this is true.** If the
table has only got as far as "it's a loop", Act Three still works: the letter
corrects them, and being corrected by the letter is a better scene than
working it out.

**Undelivered revelations** fold into the letter. Note them on your sheet now,
before you start Act Three.

## ACT THREE — *The Order* *(~30 minutes)*

**A decision, not a die roll.** `[CoC: no roll resolves Act Three. Say so at
the table if anyone reaches for dice.]`

### Opening (read aloud)

> It is a quarter past three. The lighter is at the steps and Mr Pike is
> drinking tea out of a tin on the stair. The fog has not lifted and will not
> now. You are standing in a brick room with a hundred and one open crates and
> one closed one, and between you, you have the authority to decide what
> happens to vault 114 — which means, although nobody has written it down
> anywhere, that you have the authority to decide what happens to Peter
> Shearing.

### The letter (optional, and the best thing in the scenario)

If the players write to the Shadwell receiving-house, the reply is in their
hands by the next morning — or, if the table wants it today, a boy brings it to
the wharf at 3:20, which is impossible and which nobody will question.

**Hand over `H3-A`. Do not read it aloud.** Let one player read it to the
others; the pause while they skim it first is the scene.

> **`H3-A` — Shearing's reply**
>
> *Sir, or Madam, as the case may be —*
>
> *I have your letter, which is the first I have had, and I have read it four
> times in order to be certain of that.*
>
> *You ask what the crates are. They are a record of the distance. I began them
> in the belief that I should want to know how far I had come, and I have kept
> them in the belief that someone might one day want to know the same, and I
> see now that these were two different beliefs and that only the second was
> any use.*
>
> *You ask whether it is the same day. It is. It is also not. I am not able to
> put that better and I have had some time to try.*
>
> *You ask what I want. I am obliged to you for asking, and I find I must
> decline to answer it, because it is not a question I have any right to put to
> four strangers on a Thursday, and because I have observed that a man who asks
> is a man who is answered once and then avoided. The order is paid to June. My
> affairs are in order. The last crate is labelled.*
>
> *I would not have you think I am complaining. The tide here is always
> correct, and that has been a greater comfort than you would suppose.*
>
> *Your obedient servant,*
>
> *P. Shearing, clerk*
>
> *Direction as before, if there should be occasion. There will not, I think,
> be occasion.*

**Improv extensions, if the table writes a second time:** he answers any
factual question precisely and briefly. He will not answer *what are you*, *are
you dead*, or *do you want to be let go* — not evasively, but by declining, in
one line, with an apology. **He never asks for anything.** If a player
explicitly offers to take the order on, he writes back: *"I should not have
asked you. I did not ask you. I am obliged to you."* That is as close as he
comes.

### The Choices (present plainly, then get out of the way)

Lay all four out. None is correct and the scenario honours all of them.

| Choice | Who can do it | What it costs | What happens |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Let it lapse** | The bank's clerk, with a signature, today | Nothing, administratively. Everything, otherwise | The account closes at the June quarter. The rope goes slack. See *Lapse* below |
| **Assign the order** | Any PC with a name, a signature and an account | A commitment with no end, no explanation, and a quarterly payment forever | It works. Demonstrably. Crate 102 is still labelled with this June |
| **Reach along the rope** | Anybody with pen and paper | Nothing — and it changes nothing by itself | The terms become known. This does not resolve the vault; it resolves the *table* |
| **Clear the vault and go** | All of them, as instructed | The job is done correctly | Pike loads a hundred and two crates. The paperwork is clean. Crate 102 is still filling |

**Compromise endings players will invent — all legitimate, write the one they
pick into the Epilogue:**
- **Pay one more quarter and decide later.** Available, cheap, and the most
  human answer. The order renews to September. Nobody has resolved anything,
  and everyone knows it.
- **Assign it to the dock company** as a sitting tenant's charge on the
  demolition. Clever, clerical, entirely in genre, and it works for as long as
  the dock company exists — which the surveyor can tell them is not forever.
- **Split it four ways** between the player characters, a shilling or two each
  per quarter. This is the best ending in the scenario and the one most tables
  will find. Let them.
- **Take the crates, leave the order running.** The vault empties, the rent
  keeps being paid on an empty room, and nothing is said.
- **Write to Shearing and burn the letter unopened.** Someone will suggest it.
  Let them, and let the player who suggested it be the one holding `H3-B` at
  the end.

### If someone shoots

Nobody can be shot. The chaos contingency here is **destruction**: a player
burns the crates, smashes crate 102, or takes a chisel to the brick.

- **Burning the crates** is a fire on a bonded wharf. Pike and Garrow will stop
  them, physically and without violence, because it is their licence and their
  livelihood. If they somehow succeed: the crates burn, the order is still
  paid, and crate 102 is not among them, because it is not where they left it.
- **Smashing crate 102** — it is two boards and a batten and it comes apart at
  once. Underneath is the label, intact, on the floor. Hand over `H3-B`
  anyway. A broken crate is still a crate and he will knock up another.
- **The brick** — eighteen inches of 1829 London stock, and no.

Give the destroying table a real ending: theirs is the **Pyrrhic** outcome
below, and it is written, and it is not a punishment.

### Crate 102 — the last beat of the act

Whatever they decide, end the act here. Not before.

> The last crate is not in a stack. It is standing by itself against the far
> wall, lid loose, knocked together out of two boards and a batten by somebody
> who had done it a hundred and one times before. It is empty. Someone has
> written the label already.

**Hand over `H3-B`.** It reads:

> **114 — JUNE 1894 — JUNE 1895**
> *contents to follow*

Say nothing after that. Let the table do it.

### Pacing Note — Act Three

**Must be true before the Epilogue:** the decision is made and *owned* — you
can name which player chose it.

**If you are behind:** cut the letter. Say so to yourself first — it is the
best scene in the game and losing it is a real loss, so cut it only if you are
past 3:05. Then the choices take four minutes and crate 102 takes two.

**If the table cracks it early** (you are at 2:30 and the decision is made):
expand the per-PC vignettes, then run **The Tangent**.

**No new information in this act.** If you catch yourself revealing something,
it belonged in Act Two.

## Epilogue — *Contents to Follow* *(~15 minutes)*

### Outcomes

| Outcome | Condition | What it reads like |
|---|---|---|
| **Lapse** | The order is allowed to fail | The June quarter passes. The crates, wherever they are, are correct up to crate 101 and stop. No crate 102 is ever filled, by anybody. Nobody can prove a man was lost, because nobody can prove a man was there. The vault's brick is in the foundations of a warehouse in Wapping and the warehouse is fine |
| **Continuance** | The order is assigned, split, or paid on | It works. The next quarter is paid. In June a crate is filed somewhere, by someone, and the player who signed will spend the rest of their life knowing the date. There is no reward and no thanks and the tide is still correct |
| **Clearance** | The vault is emptied as instructed, order untouched | A hundred and two crates go up the river on the afternoon tide. The dock company's instruction is discharged. The rent on an empty room continues to be paid by a standing order nobody can explain, and in June crate 102 begins to fill — with the papers of a Thursday in February 1895, in Wapping, four people and a chisel |
| **Pyrrhic** | The crates destroyed, or crate 102 taken as a souvenir | The record is gone and the arrangement is not. Crate 102 is knocked up again out of two boards and a batten. The hand is a little worse than it was |

### Per-PC vignettes (keyed to outcome)

Give each player one. Second person, three sentences, no more.

- **The bank's clerk.** Whatever he signed, he filed it, because that is the
  job. *Continuance:* the quarterly entry appears in his own day-book in his
  own hand, and he has started checking it against the tide. *Lapse:* the
  dormancy flag is withdrawn for the sixty-second time, by a junior, in error,
  and he lets it stand.
- **The wharfinger.** He walks the corridor at the right speed now, and has
  worked out why he used not to. The dog still will not go past the door, and
  there is no longer a door.
- **The searcher.** The title is clean, the chain of custody is immaculate, and
  there is one endorsement in the chain in a hand a hundred years old that he
  has decided not to raise with anybody.
- **The surveyor.** He valued the contents at eleven shillings as pulp. He put
  it in the report. The report was accepted, and he has kept the coin.
- **The newspaperman** *(if played)*. He has the story of the century and no
  second source, and he has worked out that a story with no second source is
  not a story. He writes four hundred words about the demolition of a bonded
  wharf instead, and they are good.
- **The lighterman** *(if played)*. He has carried stranger cargo. He has not
  carried quieter.

### Table debrief

One question, then stop: **"Whose papers are in crate 102?"** Do not answer
it.

### Closing Scene (read aloud, adapt to outcome)

> The fog goes off the river some time after dark and the wharf stands out of
> it, four floors of blackened brick and a crane arm, for the last few weeks it
> has. Somewhere up the reach the tide turns when the table says it will turn.
> On the wall of a brick room, eight feet by twelve, there is a card in a clerk's
> careful hand, and underneath the date it says *contents to follow*, and it is
> quite right.

## The Tangent

*A bonus scene for a table that finishes early. Already written; run it after
the vignettes.*

**The chandler's cellar.** The site of crates 1–34, pulled down in 1828 and
built over by a public house that is still there, two streets away, and open.
The cellar is the pub's cellar now. It is a brick barrel-vault, it is dry, and
it has no echo.

The landlord will show them for the price of a drink. He mentions, because it
is the only interesting thing about his cellar, that the brewery's draymen will
not leave the hatch open, and that he has never got a straight answer as to
why. There is nothing down there. There is a space against the far wall,
roughly eight feet by twelve, where nothing has ever been stacked.

Let the players stand in it. Then call time.

## Open questions — **resolved for v1**

1. **Three inventory handouts or ten?** → **Three printed** (`H2-A`, `H2-B`,
   `H2-C`), the rest answered from the **Drift Table**. Ten is better horror
   and a real print cost; the Drift Table does the same work for free and
   handles crates the players name that no handout anticipated.
2. **Does Shearing's reply get a script or improv notes?** → **A script, in
   full** (`H3-A`), plus improv rules for a second letter. The flatness risk is
   real and is answered by making the letter *short and specific* and by never
   reading it aloud — the players read it themselves.
3. **Wapping, or a provincial port?** → **Wapping.** London is richer, the fog
   is free, and the river is load-bearing: the tide-tables being eternally
   correct is the scenario's one piece of consolation.
4. **Is a gentle horror with no antagonist enough for 3h30?** → **One hard
   beat, and it is a false alarm** (the dog, at 1:50, planted in Act One).
   Its purpose is to establish that this game does not do jump scares, so that
   the following hour of nothing is credible rather than merely empty.

### New open questions, raised by writing v1

1. **Does the 9am Wednesday slot want a colder open?** Design intent 2 argues
   the quiet is a courtesy to an arriving table. Untested. If the first playtest
   drags in the first twenty minutes, the fix is to open *inside* the corridor
   with the chisel already in hand and tell Act One's document work in
   flashback — but that costs the clerical pleasure the whole game is built on.
2. **Six sheets and one room.** Six investigators in an eight-by-twelve vault
   is physically crowded, which is good, and conversationally crowded, which is
   not. Watch whether the sort in Movement Two becomes four people working and
   two people watching.
3. **Is the split-four-ways ending too easy?** It is the ending most tables will
   reach and it costs almost nothing. That may be correct — a shared, cheap,
   permanent obligation is a fine note to end on — or it may need the
   solicitor's searcher to point out that an undocumented perpetual charge
   shared between four strangers is, legally, nothing at all.

## GM Notes (private)

- **The scenario's failure mode is impatience** — the GM's, not the table's.
  Ninety minutes of reading paper feels, from behind the screen, like nothing
  is happening. Something is: they are building the drift. Do not add a noise.
- **Let them be wrong about the loop for at least twenty minutes.** The
  correction is the scenario's best structural move and it has to be earned.
- **Shearing is not a ghost and is not a victim.** He is a competent clerk in
  an impossible situation who has kept his records in order for a century and
  has decided, on principle, not to be a burden. Play him as dignified. If he
  reads as pitiable the ending stops working, because pity makes the decision
  easy and the decision must not be easy.
- **Nobody in this scenario is lying.** Every document is true. That is where
  the horror comes from.
