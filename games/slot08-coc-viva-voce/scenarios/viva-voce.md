## Overview

- **Event:** Contingency 2027 — Friday 29 January, Slot 8, afternoon,
  Searles Leisure Resort, Hunstanton.
- **System:** Call of Cthulhu 7e — **classic 1920s.** Miskatonic University,
  Arkham, Massachusetts. **Friday 11 March 1927.**
- **Setting:** A department, a library, a sanatorium, and one examination room.
- **Tone:** Dry, courteous, procedural, and then not. The slate's only 1920s
  game and **the only one set on the Mythos' home ground** — it should feel
  like the others are visiting.
- **Maximum Runtime:** 3 hours 30 minutes play + 10 minute break
- **Players:** 4–6. Six sheets; runs at four. Drop the visiting engineer first,
  then the mathematics instructor — **and note what that does to the vote**
  (below).
- **Content warnings:** as in GM Essentials. Safety tools in use.
- **Art Style:** Ink sketch / pencil illustration. Direction: **paper, rooms
  and water.** A bound thesis, a card catalogue, a committee table, the valley
  in rain. **Nothing is depicted that the thesis describes.**

### Design intent (read this first)

1. **The thesis is the monster, and the players must read it.** Four extracts,
   handed out across the session. They are dry, careful, well-argued, and **in
   order they do something.** Nothing else in the scenario is as important as
   getting those four pages right.
2. **No combat, and nothing to shoot.** This is a room, a document, and a vote.
3. **The horror is procedural.** The question is never *what is it* but **what
   does the committee do at three o'clock** — and the committee is the players.
4. **Ardwin Pell is not a villain and must not be played as one.** He is a very
   good student who found a real pattern. Every instinct the table has to make
   him sinister should be met with politeness and a reasonable answer.
5. **The climax is a vote, and every way it goes costs something.**

### The vote — how it works

Adopted from the roster, which worked it out from the PC side; **this file is
the authority and this is the frame.**

**Statute XI:** a Chair (Prof. Lyle, NPC) plus three to five Examiners. Dr Sze
is an NPC examiner. The Registrar's clerk and the Keeper of the Collection
**attend and do not vote.** Simple majority of Examiners; the Chair votes only
to break a tie.

| Players | PC examiner votes | Of | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **6** | 4 | 5 | The PCs can carry any outcome by themselves |
| **5** | 3 | 5 | A majority, with no margin — one defection decides it |
| **4** | 2 | 4 | **Winning Dr Sze in Act Two becomes mandatory, not optional** |

**Two sheets never drop** — the Keeper and the secretary — because they hold
the catalogue and the minute, and neither has a vote. That is deliberate: the
two players who know most have to persuade rather than decide.

**Period manners, and keep them.** Examiners question in seniority order. A
junior member waits for the Chair's invitation. Persons in attendance do not
speak unless asked. **This is not decoration — it is the obstacle.** The two
PCs who know the most are the two who may not speak, and getting the truth into
that room past Statute XI is the real climax.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

Pell's thesis is not *about* anything. It is **a procedure wearing a
dissertation's clothes.**

The argument is genuinely sound — the hydrology is real, the fieldwork was
done, the valley does drain the way he says. But the **order** of the argument,
the recursion in chapters nine through fourteen, and the way each section
requires the reader to hold the previous section's conclusion in mind *while
provisionally accepting its negation*, constitute a method. Read in sequence,
attentively, by someone trained to follow an argument, **it installs a way of
looking at moving water.** After that, the valley looks back.

Pell did not invent it. He found it in the **field notes of a surveyor named
Corliss Amory**, who walked the valley in 1851 and drowned in nine inches of
it. Pell's real achievement — and he is quietly proud of it — is that he worked
out what Amory's notes were *for*, and completed the argument Amory could not
finish.

**The three examiners:**

- **Prof. Josiah Hark**, 58 — read it in two sittings. He is in Arkham
  Sanatorium and has filled **one hundred and ten pages with the same four
  sentences.** He is the warning, he is beyond help, and **the scenario must
  not offer a cure.**
- **Dr Eleanor Sze**, 41 — read it over a fortnight, with breaks. She has lost
  one sound: **she cannot hear running water, at all**, and has not noticed
  that this is a mercy. The scenario's best source and most reliable witness.
- **Prof. Bertram Lyle**, 63 — skimmed it, as he skims everything. He is fine.
  He intends to vote to pass, because the hydrology is sound, Hark's collapse
  is obviously unrelated, and the department needs a doctorate this year.

**The library has already catalogued it.** A bound copy went to the Restricted
Collection on the 4th as a matter of routine, and **two borrowing slips have
been filled in since.** That is the Countdown: this stopped being a question
about one document a week ago.

### Revelations (GM checklist)

1. The thesis is excellent by every normal measure.
2. The chapter order in the bound copy is not the order in the submitted
   abstract.
3. Two works in the bibliography do not exist — **and one of them is dated
   1928.**
4. Hark read it in two sittings and was committed the next day.
5. Hark has written the same four sentences one hundred and ten times.
6. Sze read it slowly, with breaks, and is largely intact.
7. **Sze cannot hear running water**, and has not worked out that this is a
   mercy.
8. The thesis is not about drainage. **It is a method, and reading it in order
   is performing it.**
9. The method came from Amory's 1851 field notes.
10. Amory drowned in nine inches of water.
11. **A copy was catalogued into the Restricted Collection on the 4th.**
12. **Two slips since.** One in the Keeper's hand against a committed man's
    name; one signed by a living student who is reading it now.
13. Pell knows exactly what he has and is proud of it — **and is not wrong that
    he has earned a doctorate.**

**Undelivered by Act Three?** Dr Sze states 6, 7, 8 and 11 plainly — see her
pacing lever. Act Three has **no new information.**

## Player Experience

You have a free afternoon and a favour to do: sit on a viva for a colleague who
is unwell. You read the thesis the night before, because that is what one does,
and it is good — tight, patient, almost beautiful about silt.

By lunchtime you have found that the bibliography cites two works that do not
exist, that one of them is dated next year, and that the chapter order in the
bound copy is not the chapter order in the submitted abstract. By two you have
spoken to a man who cannot stop writing and a woman who cannot hear a bell.

At three you sit down opposite a polite, nervous, entirely likeable young man
who answers every question you have correctly, and at some point in the next
hour the five of you have to raise your hands.

### `H0-A` — EXTRACT ONE: the abstract *(Introduction)*

> **ON THE DRAINAGE OF THE MISKATONIC VALLEY, WITH PARTICULAR REFERENCE TO
> SEASONAL DISCHARGE AND THE BEHAVIOUR OF MINOR WATERCOURSES**
> *A. Pell · submitted January 1927 · 406 pp.*
>
> This thesis establishes, from four seasons of gauged measurement at
> thirty-one stations, that the valley's minor watercourses do not drain
> independently of one another, as the standard account holds, but behave as a
> single distributed system whose response to rainfall is both slower and more
> regular than their separate catchments would predict.
>
> Chapters one to eight set out the measurements. Chapters nine to fourteen
> develop the argument from them. Chapter fifteen considers objections.
>
> *The reader is asked to take the chapters in the order given.*

**Hand it out in the Introduction with no comment.** It is unremarkable, which
is the point, and the last line is the only thing in it that matters.

---

### `H1-A` — EXTRACT TWO: the bibliography page *(Act One)*

A page of fifty-one entries, correctly formatted, almost all real. Among them:

> AMORY, C. *Field notes, Miskatonic valley, 1849–51.* MS, uncatalogued.
> Miskatonic University Library.
>
> FENN, R. H. *The Regulation of Minor Watercourses in New England.* Boston,
> 1902.
>
> **HARK, J. *On a Method of Reading.* Arkham, 1928.**
>
> MARCHETTI, G. *Studi sui depositi alluvionali.* Firenze, 1911.
>
> **PELL, A. *Completion.* n.p., n.d.**

`[CoC: Library Use — Fenn and Marchetti are real and in the stacks. Amory is
real and uncatalogued, which is itself a finding. Neither Hark 1928 nor Pell
*Completion* exists in any catalogue anywhere.]`

**The dating error is the Act One checkpoint.** It is 11 March 1927.

---

### `H2-A` — EXTRACT THREE: from chapter twelve *(Act Two)*

**This is the one that should make a player stop reading aloud.** Print it
alone on one side of a sheet, in a serious typeface, with a page number.

> §12.4. It will be convenient at this point to recall the conclusion of §9.2 —
> that the minor watercourses respond as one — and to hold it in mind while
> setting it aside, since the measurements of the following section are
> intelligible only to a reader prepared to entertain its negation without
> abandoning it.
>
> The reader who has followed the argument in the order given will by now have
> acquired the habit of this double attention, and will be applying it without
> effort, as one applies a grammar.
>
> It is worth remarking that the habit, once acquired, is not confined to the
> page. The reader will find that moving water is from now on legible in the
> same manner: that a stream read attentively resolves into a statement whose
> first clause is its discharge and whose second clause is its direction, and
> that the two together constitute an address.
>
> Addresses, of course, imply correspondents. §12.5 sets out the gauged figures
> for the Garrison Brook.

**Sanity 1/1D4** for any investigator who reads it attentively. `[CoC: no roll
avoids this. Reading it is the mechanism; a player who skims aloud and stops
halfway takes nothing, and that is a legitimate outcome — say nothing about
it.]`

**Why it works and what not to do:** the horror is entirely in the register.
It never raises its voice, it never addresses the reader as *you*, and the
worst sentence in it is about grammar. **Do not add sound effects, do not
dim the lights, do not pause meaningfully.** Hand it over and let somebody
read it.

---

### `H3-A` — EXTRACT FOUR: Pell's own summary *(Act Three, conditional)*

**Only if somebody asks him to explain chapter twelve.** He will be pleased to
be asked — it is the chapter he is proudest of — and he will hand over a single
paragraph in his own handwriting, which he keeps in the front of his copy.

> *What Amory could not do was finish it. He had the whole of it by 1850 and he
> spent the last year trying to write the final section and could not, and I
> think I know why: he was trying to describe the valley, and the valley is not
> the subject. The reader is the subject. Once I understood that the thing
> wanted a reader and not a description, the last six chapters took eleven
> weeks. I do not expect the committee to find this part interesting. It is the
> only part I am proud of.*

**Sanity 1/1D2** for anyone who reads it during the viva, **and the vote
happens anyway, in the next ten minutes, with that in their hand.**

## ACT TWO — *The Afternoon* *(~90 minutes)*

**Shape:** Sze and what she cannot hear. Amory's notes in the stacks. The

**Splits:** **Track A — the library** (Amory, the catalogue, the slips, the

### Dr Eleanor Sze — the scenario's best source

*Essence: cannot hear running water; wants the committee to do the thing she
could not do alone.*

Forty-one, precise, tired, entirely sane. She read it over a fortnight **with
breaks**, which is why she is intact, and she worked it out a week ago and has
**written nothing down** — deliberately.

**The demonstration.** Take her to a window overlooking the Miskatonic, or run
a tap. She will look at it and say something accurate about the flow and be
completely unaware there is any sound. A bell, a voice, a dropped book: all
fine. **Running water: nothing at all.**

> *"I lost it on about the ninth day and I did not notice for four more, which
> I find is the part people are least comfortable with. It is not deafness. I
> can hear the tap in the sense that I know the tap is on. There is simply
> nothing coming in. And I will tell you the thing I have not said to anybody:
> I think it is a kindness. I think something stopped a door, and I think it
> stopped it from my side."*

**Her position:** Pell has earned a doctorate. The thesis must not be
published, catalogued, or lent. **She does not know how to have both**, and
she is hoping the committee is cleverer than she is.

**Pacing lever:** if the clock has beaten the table, Sze says all of it plainly
and asks what the committee intends to do. **She exists so Act Three is never
unreachable.**

### The library — Track A

**Amory's field notes**, uncatalogued since 1888, in a box in the stacks. Two
hundred pages of competent survey work, and then the last eighteen months.

`[CoC: Library Use — the notes are a draft of the same argument, in the same
order, and they stop mid-sentence in the final section]`

**Amory's last entry**, 4 April 1851:

> *I have been at the last part eleven months and I cannot write it. I believe
> now that I have been going at it the wrong way about. It does not want
> describing.*

**Sanity 0/1.** He drowned nine days later in nine inches of water at the ford
below Garrison Brook, which is the brook from §12.5.

### Pell in the corridor

He is outside the examination room from half past two, in a borrowed gown, and
he will talk to anybody.

*Essence: found something real and finished it properly; wants the doctorate he
has honestly earned.*

Twenty-four, nervous in the ordinary way, courteous, and **he answers every

February, and he explains why in terms of exposition and is right. Asked about

intends to write and he knows he should have removed it. Asked about Amory: he
lights up.

**Every instinct the table has to make him sinister must be met with
politeness and a reasonable answer.**

## ACT THREE — *The Viva* *(~30 minutes)*

**Three o'clock. No new information. Questions, answers, and raised hands.**

### Opening (read aloud)

> The examination room has a long table, eight chairs, a carafe of water that
> nobody will touch, and a window that looks down College Street to the bridge
> and the brown water going under it. Pell comes in, shakes hands with
> everybody including the clerk, sits down with his copy squared in front of
> him, and waits to be asked a question. Prof. Lyle looks at the clock, and
> then at the committee, and says: *"Well. Shall we begin? Dr Keel, you're
> senior."*

### How to run the viva

**Run it as an actual viva** for ten to twelve minutes before anybody mentions
anything strange. Let the players ask about hydrology. Pell is excellent on
hydrology.

**Statute XI is the obstacle.** The Keeper and the secretary know the most and
**may not speak unless asked.** Watch what the table does about that — the
written routes are:

- An examiner asks the Keeper a direct question, which is permitted and which
  any PC examiner can do at any time.
- The secretary writes something on the minute and turns it round.
- Someone breaks the statute out loud, in front of the Chair, and wears it.
- **Lyle can be worked**, before three, into inviting them.

**Lyle intends to pass him.** He says so in the first two minutes, pleasantly,
as though it were settled. He is not stupid: shown the 1928 citation he calls
it sloppy; shown Hark's pages he says Hark has been unwell for two years;
shown Extract Three he reads four lines of it, says *"yes, it's dense,"* and
puts it down. **He skims. That is his whole character and it is also his
protection.**

### Extract Four

**Only if someone asks Pell to explain chapter twelve.** Hand over `H3-A`.
**Sanity 1/1D2**, and the vote happens anyway, in the next ten minutes, with
that paragraph in somebody's hand.

### The vote

Go round the table. **Every player says which way and why, out loud, in
character.** Form 14 is signed and goes back to the Registrar by five.

| Ending | What it takes | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Passed** | A majority | The degree is awarded, and the thesis is bound, catalogued and lent. **The epilogue is a borrowing slip with a new name on it** |
| **Failed** | A majority | A good young man is finished on grounds nobody will put in writing. The thesis stays in the Restricted Collection — **where the other two copies already are** |
| **Referred back** | A majority | Twelve months of revisions. The problem belongs to next year's committee, and the table will know exactly what they have done |
| **Withdrawn** | Pell agrees to pull it himself | The hardest to earn and **the only ending he participates in.** He will ask, reasonably, what he is supposed to do instead — and somebody has to answer him |

**Compromise endings players will invent:**
- **Pass him and restrict the thesis permanently.** Available, and it is what
  Sze wants. It requires the Keeper, who has no vote, and a Library Board
  minute that does not exist yet.
- **Pass him on condition he never publishes.** He will agree. He is
  twenty-four and he will agree to anything today, and in 1931 he will be
  thirty-five and will not.
- **Fail him and hire him.** Grotesque, practical, and the department would
  wear it.
- **Go and take the book off Agnes Thurlow first.** The best thing any table
  can do and it is not on this table, because it is not a vote. If somebody
  leaves the room at three o'clock to do it, **let them**, and let the vote
  happen without them.

### If someone destroys the thesis

There are **three bound copies**, Pell's handwritten draft, and Amory's notes
in the stacks. Burning one is a gesture. The scenario treats it as one, **and
the vote still happens at three.**

### Pacing Note — Act Three

**Must be true before the Epilogue:** a vote was taken and every player said
which way and why.

**If you are behind:** Lyle calls the vote at 3:05 whether the questioning is
finished or not. That is what chairs do.

## Epilogue — *The Slip* *(~15 minutes)*

### Outcomes

| Outcome | What it reads like |
|---|---|
| **Passed** | Dr Pell is appointed to a junior lectureship at a small college in Vermont and is well liked. The thesis is bound in three further copies and placed on open shelves in September |
| **Failed** | He takes it badly and quietly. He is working in his father's firm in Providence by June. **He has his handwritten draft, and eleven weeks of free evenings every winter** |
| **Referred back** | A year of revisions. He is thorough. The second version is two hundred pages longer and the chapter order is the same |
| **Withdrawn** | He withdraws, and asks what he is supposed to do instead, and whoever answered him will remember answering him |
| **Pyrrhic** | A copy burned, Sze overruled, Thurlow not reached | Everything that was true at three o'clock is still true, and now nobody is watching it |

### Per-PC vignettes

- **The geologist.** He saw an anomaly in the field in 1921 and wrote
  *instrument error* in his own book. Whatever he voted, he has got the book
  out again.
- **The medical officer.** He stopped reading at page forty to assess Hark, and
  voted as the only honest man in the room who had not read the exhibit. He has
  not finished it. **He has not thrown it away either.**
- **The mathematics instructor.** Her own thesis was referred back twice with
  no reason given. She knows precisely what *referred back* costs, and she is
  the only person in that room who did.
- **The Keeper.** He catalogued it in nine minutes on the 4th and filled in the
  slip of the 6th himself. He is the reason it is in the world, and he said so
  or he did not, in a room where he was not permitted to speak.
- **The secretary.** She typed the fair copy in January — four hundred and six
  pages, in order, attentively. **Nobody has asked her how she is.** Write her
  vignette last.
- **The visiting engineer** *(if played)*. He came a day early because Hark
  asked him to in a letter dated 28 February, and the fourth line of that
  letter is the only thing anyone wrote down in advance.

### Table debrief

One question: **"Who is reading it now?"** Do not answer it.

### Closing Scene (read aloud, adapt)

> The Registrar's office closes at five and Form 14 goes into the file with the
> others, and that is the end of it administratively. In the Restricted
> Collection the card for Pell, A., *On the Drainage of the Miskatonic Valley*,
> has two slips clipped to it and room for a great many more. Outside, the
> river is high and brown and perfectly ordinary, and goes under the bridge at
> the bottom of College Street making a sound that most people can hear.

**Then read the last line flat:**

> *Third slip. Dated the fourteenth of March. Signed in a hand you recognise.*

Do not say whose. Let the table decide which of them it is.

## The Tangent

*For a table that finishes early.*

**The thirty-one gauging stations.** Pell's appendix lists them with grid
references, and somebody will want to visit one. Station 14 is a concrete post
with a brass plate and a staff gauge in the Garrison Brook, forty minutes out
of Arkham, and it is a perfectly ordinary piece of hydrological apparatus.

The brook is nine inches deep over gravel. The staff gauge reads nine inches.
The figure in the thesis for 11 March, written in January, is nine inches.

Let them stand in it. Then call time.

## Open questions — **resolved for v1**

1. **How far does Extract Three go?** → **Written in full above**, and its
   limits are stated: it never raises its voice, never addresses the reader as
   *you*, and its worst sentence is about grammar. The GM is explicitly told
   not to add atmosphere to it. **It still wants a desk playtest with someone
   reading it cold** — that is the one test this scenario cannot skip.
2. **Are the two borrowers named or blank?** → **Both named, and better than
   the outline's lean.** The slip of the 6th is in the **Keeper's own hand
   against the committed Hark's name**, which makes it a PC's seam rather than
   a lead; the slip of the 9th is **Agnes Thurlow**, who is alive, findable, and
   reading it right now. The Countdown gets a face.
3. **Does Hark get dialogue?** → **No. Pages only.** He is more frightening as
   handwriting than as a man who explains, and the scenario does not offer a
   cure. One written exception exists for a stalled table, and it is a line he
   writes, not says.
4. **The slate's only 1920s game.** → Its manners are the obstacle, not the
   decoration: Statute XI means the two players who know the most are the two
   who may not speak, and getting the truth past that is the real climax.

### New open questions, raised by writing v1

1. **Does the viva survive ten minutes of actual hydrology?** The act is
   written to run as a real examination before anything strange is raised. If a
   convention table finds that dull rather than excruciating, the fix is to
   have Lyle call for the vote much earlier and make the whole act a fight to
   delay it.
2. **Agnes Thurlow may be too good an answer.** Fetching the book off her is
   obviously right, costs nothing, and is available from 2:10. If every
   playtest table simply does it, she needs to be harder to reach — a locked
   reading room, or an essay deadline that makes her refuse.
3. **Statute XI at four players.** With two of four votes and two non-voting
   sheets, the PCs cannot carry the vote without Sze. That is written as
   deliberate, but it means a four-player table that mishandles Sze has no
   route to its preferred ending, which may read as the scenario being unfair
   rather than tight.

## GM Notes (private)

- **Pell is innocent and correct.** He found a real thing, did the fieldwork,
  and wrote it up properly. If the table ends up hating him, somebody has
  played him wrong. The tragedy only works if passing him is genuinely the
  just outcome.
- **Do not perform Extract Three.** Hand it to a player. The silence after they
  stop reading is the scenario, and you cannot produce it by acting.
- **Hark is beyond help and the scenario must not flinch from that.** No cure,
  no lucid moment, no final message that helps. He read it in two sittings.
- **Sze is right about everything and still cannot solve it.** She is what a
  competent, careful, decent person looks like at the end of the argument,
  which is why she is the scenario's conscience rather than its answer.
- **The vote is the horror.** Five people with a form, a deadline of five
  o'clock, and no option that is clean.
