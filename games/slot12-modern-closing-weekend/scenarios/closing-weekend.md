## GM ESSENTIALS (one glance, any GM)

- **System crash-course:** Call of Cthulhu 7e, **modern rules.** d100 under the
  skill; under half hard, under a fifth extreme. **01 is a critical. 100 is
  always a fumble; if the skill is under 50, 96–00 fumbles.**
  **Push:** repeat the attempt with more effort, once; a failed push earns a
  real consequence. **Luck:** spend Luck 1-for-1 to turn a failure into a
  success — **but not on a Sanity roll, not on a damage roll, and not on a roll
  you have already pushed. One or the other, never both.** Criticals and
  fumbles stand and cannot be bought off.
- **Mechanics inline:** `[CoC: Skill — what it does]`
- **READ THIS BEFORE PAGE TWO: the killer wins fights and the players lose
  them.** He is not a stat block to be solved with a deer rifle. **There are no
  pursuit rolls — the GM places him**, and he is always closer than the
  geometry says. Say this to the table in the Introduction so nobody discovers
  it by surprise.
- **Nobody dies on one failed roll.** Death comes on the **second** failure in
  a scene; the GM narrates the first as a near miss.
- **Sanity is not the dial in this scenario — the killer is.** Charge the two
  written rolls (1/1D6 at the first body, 1/1D4 at the unmasking) and **nothing
  else.** Do not apply the loss table for witnessed deaths — the book prices a
  friend's violent death at 0/1D6 and this scenario expects three to six of
  them, which is a different and worse game. A flat **0/1 for each death after
  the first** keeps the ledger honest, and **no second-character handover ever
  costs the incoming player Sanity.** Expect 2–6 points lost across a session
  and roughly one bout of madness at the table all night. That is deliberate.
- **No elimination.** A dead player has a second character in hand **within two
  minutes**, from a prepared set of four.
- **The clock counts play time** — pause it for the break.
- **Hard rule: at 2:35 — not 2:45 — you go to Act Three regardless.** This is
  the one game on the slate with an earlier hard stop than the house 2:45,
  because **Act Three is forty minutes, not thirty**: the round-the-table
  approach plus the scenario's own "about twenty minutes left" argument does
  not fit in thirty and the argument is the best thing in the game. Act Two is
  planned to end at **2:25**.
- **The climax is a name, not a weapon.**
- **Safety:** graphic slasher violence against young adults; sustained stalking
  and pursuit; recreational drug and alcohol use; period attitudes left in and
  not endorsed; **the near-certainty that your first character dies.** PCs are
  **18–22, not children** — say so when you pitch it. And say the consent line
  below.

### The consent line — say it, it is not a spoiler

> *"One more thing before we start. There is a killer in this scenario and
> **he is not one of your characters.** Nobody at this table is secretly the
> murderer, nobody is being asked to play a traitor, and none of your sheets
> has a hidden instruction on it. Everything on your sheet is yours."*

**Why this is in the text.** The advert deliberately no longer says a player
character is involved, which is correct as spoiler policy — but it leaves a
convention table to wonder whether they have booked hidden-traitor play. **This
line removes a false worry without giving away a single thing**: they still do
not know who, how, or why. Say it every time.

## Overview

- **Event:** Contingency 2027 — **Saturday 30 January, Slot 12, evening**,
  Searles Leisure Resort, Hunstanton.
- **System:** Call of Cthulhu 7e — **modern rules, set Saturday 6 September
  1986.**
- **Setting:** Camp Loon Point, upstate New York. The season is over, the camp
  is sold, and on Tuesday the bulldozers come for the boathouse.
- **Tone:** **Funny for ninety minutes, then not.** Nair in the shampoo, a
  stolen station wagon, and a lake going gold at six and black at eight.
- **Maximum Runtime:** 3 hours 30 minutes play + 10 minute break
- **Players:** 4–6. **Six counsellor sheets plus four second characters.** At
  five drop the grounds hand; at four drop grounds and waterfront and cut the
  Track B boathouse split. The three facts Act Three needs are each reachable
  from two directions.
- **Content warnings:** as in GM Essentials. Safety tools in use.
- **Art Style:** Ink sketch / pencil illustration. Direction: **American summer
  camp at the end of the season.** Cabins, a lake, a boathouse, a bonfire.
  **The mask may be drawn. Denny may not.**

### Design intent (read this first)

1. **It is a slasher, played knowingly, and it is funny for ninety minutes
   before it stops being funny.** **The table should be laughing when the first
   body turns up.** If Act One is played as horror the whole thing is a grind.
2. **No elimination.** Death costs you your plans, your gear and your friends —
   **never your seat.**
3. **The killer walks and the killer wins fights.** Stated on page one so no
   table discovers it by surprise.
4. **The climax is a name, not a weapon.** The mask comes off when someone who
   knew the wearer before **says his name to his face, close enough to be
   heard.** Every instinct the genre has trained into the table says *run*. Act
   Three is six people deciding, in order, whether to walk toward it.
5. **The decision after that is the real one.** A nineteen-year-old on the
   ground, two bodies in the lake, and a mask that needs a wearer. The scenario
   does not tell the table what to do with any of the three.

### Slot note — the register question, updated

The outline worried that this game had to be Thursday's relief valve between a
9am zombie game and a Berlin 1946 game. **Both halves of that are out of
date:** the slate was rebuilt on 2026-10-25, the Berlin game was cut entirely,
and this moved to **Saturday evening.**

That is a straight gain and it changes nothing about how to run it. A slasher
at 7.30 on the convention's last full evening, with a table that has been
drinking together for three days, is a considerably easier sell than the same
game at two in the afternoon. **The first ninety minutes are still written to
be funny and must still be played that way** — that was never about the slot.

## Genre rules — one side of card, read aloud in the Introduction

- **Say the line, bank the Luck.** Deliver a trope out loud and mean it —
  splitting up, *"I'll be right back"*, the fake-out scare, the shower, the
  vehicle that will not start — and **recover 1D10 Luck. Once per player per
  act.** This exists to make players volunteer for the genre instead of
  resisting it.
- **He walks.** No chase rolls. He is placed, not raced.
- **Second failure kills.** The first failure is the near miss.
- **Pushing is how people die here.** A failed push **is** your second failure.
  If you are already on your near miss, **spend Luck instead — or run.**
- **Banked Luck is how you survive this scenario. It cannot save your mind,
  only your body** — Luck buys a failed skill roll back, never a Sanity roll,
  and never a roll you have already pushed.
- **He does not stay down.** Twice. On the third, something else is wrong.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**The mask is the scenario.** It is a crude thing: split plywood backed with
canvas, bound with wire, cut flat across the eyes, cheap as a prop and ugly in
the hand. Nobody made it for the camp — **the camp was built on top of where it
was.**

**It does not possess. It persuades.** Worn, it supplies a reason, and the
reason is always the same reason and it is always a good one: **the summer must
not end.** It makes the argument patiently, in the wearer's own voice, using
the wearer's own grievances, and the wearer agrees. **It can be taken off.**
Every one of the eleven people who have worn it took it off eventually, and
then had to go on living.

**Eleven wearers since 1931.** Loon Point's history is a line of bad years at
seven-to-eleven-year intervals — 1931, 1940, 1947, 1954, 1962, 1971, 1979 —
each written up locally as a drowning, a fire, a runaway, a bear. **The camp
office keeps every one of them in the incident log because insurance requires
it.** Read in one sitting it is unmistakable.

The mask was burned in 1954 and again in 1971. It is in the boathouse
crawlspace, unburned. **Whether what came back is the same object or a new one
made by the next wearer is a question the scenario deliberately leaves
standing**; there is a 1972 receipt for plywood in the office files for the
table that wants the duller answer.

**This year's wearer is Denny Vasquez**, nineteen, third summer on staff, the
quiet kind one everyone likes and nobody watches. He found it under the
boathouse in June, pulling out a dead raccoon. **He has killed twice already:**
Marcy Okonkwo in July (recorded as a drowning) and Tad Brisbane in August
(recorded as having walked off the job, which he had threatened to do all
summer, which is why it worked). **Denny wrote both reports himself.** He is
good at the paperwork.

**Tonight is different because the camp has been sold.** The mask's one
argument has stopped being a mood and become a deadline. There is no next
summer to protect, so the logic runs out to its end: **if the summer cannot
continue, then nothing may leave the camp that would carry the end of it
away.**

**Denny is at the party until roughly 9pm** — visible, sober, helpful, the one
who fetches more wood. **He is the one the party sends to check the fuse box.**

### Revelations (GM checklist)

1. The camp is sold and comes down on Tuesday, starting with the boathouse.
2. The phone line is cut, not stormed down.
3. The station wagon will not start, and the reason is mechanical and
   deliberate.
4. Marcy drowned in July in four feet of water.
5. Tad "quit" in August, which everyone found plausible.
6. **Both reports are in the same handwriting.**
7. **The incident log shows bad years at seven-to-eleven-year intervals since
   1931.**
8. The boathouse is the camp's first building, 1923, and something is under it.
9. The mask was burned in 1954 and in 1971 and is not burned.
10. **The mask persuades; it does not possess. It can be taken off.**
11. **It comes off when someone who knew the wearer says his name to his face.**
12. **Denny is not missing. He has been here all night.**

**Undelivered by Act Three?** Hollis Yeardley says the two load-bearing things
straight — eight summers, he has read the log, and he knows what is under the
boathouse. He has no proof and the table need not believe him. Act Three has
**no new information.**

## Player Experience

You get the whole last day: closing the cabins, siphoning the camp's gas,
arguing about who drives, the lake going gold at six and black at eight. You
get the bonfire, the beer, the dare, and the bit where two of you walk off into
the trees and only one walks back.

Then the phone line is a cut cable rather than a storm, the wagon won't turn
over, and somebody is standing between cabin four and the treeline not doing
anything at all.

By midnight you will have worked out that this camp has had a bad year every
seven to eleven years since 1931, that the thing in the boathouse crawlspace is
older than the camp, and that the reason you can't find Denny is that you have
been looking at him all night.

Then you have to decide which of you says his name.

## Dying properly

**The thing that breaks slashers at a table is that a dead player is a bored
player for ninety minutes.** So:

- **Nobody dies on one failed roll.** Death is the **second** failure inside a
  scene, and the GM narrates the first as the near miss. Players see it coming,
  which is the genre.
- **Four prepared second characters**, face-down at the GM's elbow, **handed
  over within two minutes.** The roster carries each one's ARRIVAL box (read at
  the next scene transition, never mid-beat) and ONE FACT box (the player's
  first line). Nothing is left to choose at handover.
  - **Deputy Carol Prine**, 34 — out on a noise complaint nobody made.
  - **Hollis Yeardley**, 61 — camp cook, eight summers, has read the log.
  - **Niamh Stoll**, 24 — Vanterra's surveyor, site plans, **the only car that
    starts.**
  - **Peter Okonkwo**, 20 — drove up from Albany to find out how his sister
    drowned in four feet of water.
- **Each arrives knowing something the dead counsellor did not**, so a death
  moves the investigation forward instead of stalling it. **This is the engine
  of Act Two.**
- **Four, plus a rule:** if a fifth or sixth death happens, the GM hands the
  player a surviving NPC. **Keep one of the four in GM hands until late** so
  there is somewhere for a fifth death to go.
- **The scenario does not pick a final girl and the GM must not either.**

## ACT ONE — *The Last Day* *(~60 minutes)*

**Shape:** closing the camp, and **laying the geography down by using it.** The
table should be laughing. Play it as a comedy about nineteen-year-olds.

**The act's real job is the map.** Every location the players will flee through
at night must be walked in daylight, for a mundane reason:

| Place | The daylight reason to go there |
|---|---|
| **The cabins** (1–6) | Closing shutters, stripping bunks |
| **The mess hall** | The walk-in, and the beer |
| **The camp office** | The keys, the log, the typewriter, **the phone** |
| **The boathouse** | Canoes to stack. **Tuesday's first demolition** |
| **The fuse box** | Behind the mess, and it trips if you run the walk-in and the pump together |
| **The pump house** | Draining the pipes |
| **The station wagon** | **The only vehicle on the point**, and it is temperamental |
| **The one road** | Four miles to the county road |

**Establish the phone and the wagon as ordinary objects in Act One.** Both
matter later and neither should be introduced at the moment it fails.

### The day's beats

1. **Chores, badly.** Nair in the shampoo. An argument about the radio.
2. **The dare.** Somebody swims to the raft and back after being told not to.
3. **Sunset at six**, gold on the water, and the whole table should feel
   fondly about this place.
4. **The bonfire at eight.** Beer, stories, the staff Polaroid from June passed
   around.
5. **Two go into the trees. One comes back.** Nobody panics; it is funny for
   about four minutes.
6. **~1:05 — the first body.**

**Denny is present, visible and helpful all evening**, and fetches more wood
twice. When the lights go at about 1:00, **he is the one the party sends to
check the fuse box.**

End the act on it. **Not a jump scare — a discovery**, and it should land in

> He is between cabin four and the treeline, face down, and the first thing

> about. The second thing anybody says is nothing at all.

**Sanity 1/1D6.**

## ACT TWO — *The Night* *(~70 minutes)*

**Shape:** survival and investigation, splitting into **Track A (the office)**
and **Track B (the boathouse)**. First counsellor deaths; second characters
arrive carrying facts. **The road, the cable and the cars close off one at a
time**, never all at once.

### Track A — the office

- **The incident log, 1923–1986**, insurance copy. `H2-A`. **Read as a whole it
  is unmistakable** and needs no roll: 1931, 1940, 1947, 1954, 1962, 1971,
  1979. `[CoC: Accounting, Library Use, or simply reading it]`
- **Marcy's drowning report and Tad's separation notice** — `H2-C`. Same
  typewriter, **same handwriting in the signature block.** `[CoC: Spot Hidden,
  or automatic if laid side by side]`
- **The 1972 plywood receipt** — `H2-D`, for the table that wants the duller
  answer about whether it is the same mask.
- **Newspaper clippings**, 1931 and 1954 — `H2-B`. **The 1954 clipping carries
  the rule:** the wearer's brother is recorded as having *"called to him"*, and
  the wearer was found sitting down, unmasked, weeping, and alive.

### Track B — the boathouse

- The crawlspace, low and dry, under the camp's **first** building.
- **Burn marks.** Twice, decades apart, and no ash.
- What else is down there: eleven sets of initials cut into a floor joist, some
  of them very old, **and a space where something has been lying.**
- **Denny's handprints in the dust.** Recent, and from June.

## ACT THREE — *The Name* *(~40 minutes — and twenty of them are the argument)*

**No new information.** The question is **who walks forward.**

### Opening (read aloud)

> He is standing on the path between the boathouse and the water, not moving,
> not hiding, about forty feet away, and the flat place where the eyes should
> be is pointed at you. He is not coming. He has not come for about a minute
> now. Everything you have ever seen says run, and the only thing that is
> going to work is to walk up to a nineteen-year-old you have known for three
> summers, close enough that he can hear you, and say *Denny*.

### How to run it

**Go round the table in order and ask each player what their character does.**
That is the act. Walking forward is voluntary, costs nothing mechanically, and
**every instinct the evening has trained says do not.**

- `[CoC: no roll is required to say his name. There is no Persuade check, no
  opposed roll, and no POW contest. Say so out loud if anyone reaches for
  dice.]`
- **It must be someone who knew the wearer before** — any of the six
  counsellors qualifies. **Hollis qualifies** (eight summers). Prine, Niamh and
  Peter do not.
- **Close enough to be heard** means inside about ten feet. He does not attack
  while someone is walking toward him and talking. **He waits.** That is the
  most frightening thing he does all night.
- **If someone runs instead**, he takes them, and the next player is asked the
  same question.

### When the mask comes off

> It is not dramatic. He stops, and he puts a hand up and takes it off the way
> you take off a baseball cap, and he is holding it, and he is nineteen and he
> is crying and he says *"it's Tuesday"* — and then he sits down on the path
> because his legs have gone.

**Sanity 1/1D4**, and then the real argument begins, with about twenty minutes
left:

**On the ground:** a boy who has killed three or four people tonight and two in
the summer, who is nineteen, who is not possessed and never was, and who agreed.
**In the lake:** Marcy and Tad. **In somebody's hands:** the mask. **At the
gate:** Niamh's car, which works.

### The Choices

| Ending | What it takes | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Said** | Someone says the name | Then: what goes in Niamh's car, who tells Deputy Prine what, and **what happens Tuesday when a bulldozer puts a blade through that crawlspace** |
| **Burned** | A fire, a third time | The table will feel it has won and **the Epilogue will not contradict them.** 1993 is not their problem |
| **Taken** | Somebody drives it out | **Whether it works away from the point is the one thing the scenario will not answer** — not in the Epilogue, not anywhere |
| **Worn** | A survivor puts it on, on purpose, with a reason they can state | **Written as a real ending and played with respect, not as a punishment.** Ask them for the reason and let them say it |

**Compromise endings players will invent:**
- **Hand Denny to Prine and say nothing about the mask.** He will be tried as a
  nineteen-year-old who killed five people, and he will not contradict a word
  of it.
- **Leave it in the crawlspace and say nothing.** Tuesday is in two days.
- **Take Denny with them and leave the mask.** The warmest instinct at the
  table and the one that leaves the object for the demolition crew.
- **Put it back deliberately and tell Vanterra not to dig.** Niamh can actually
  do this. It is the most competent answer available and it holds for about
  four years.

### If someone shoots

**Denny can be killed.** He is nineteen and he is not armoured.

> **If they shoot him — the ruling.** He has no stat block and needs none.
> **The first hit puts him down and he gets up.** That is one of his two.
> **The second puts him down and he gets up.** That is the other. **A third hit
> kills him.** No roll decides whether a hit "counts": a successful Firearms
> roll is a hit, and a hit is one of the three. He carries **a fire axe from
> the boathouse** and **does not raise it while anybody is talking to him.**

**The mask comes off a body as easily as a face** — and then it is in the room
with the people who have just shot their friend, and it is very reasonable, and
it has all night.

### Pacing Note — Act Three

**Must be true before the Epilogue:** somebody walked forward or nobody did,
and what happened to the mask is decided out loud.

**If behind:** skip straight to the path at 2:50 and go round the table once.

## The Tangent

*For a table that finishes early.*

**The 1979 wearer is alive.** The log gives a name, and a second character —
Prine, or Hollis — can place her: she is thirty-nine, she runs a hardware store
in Oneonta, and she took the mask off herself in September 1979 after eleven
days.

She will talk, on the phone, at four in the morning, to strangers, immediately,
as though she has been waiting seven years for the call. She answers every
question plainly and without self-pity.

The last thing she says before she hangs up is that the argument it makes is a
good argument, and that she has never been able to explain to anybody why it is
wrong, and that she would rather they did not call again.

## Open questions — **resolved for v1, except one that is deliberately open**

1. **Four second characters or six?** → **Four**, plus a written rule: the GM
   keeps one of the four in NPC hands until late so a fifth death has somewhere
   to go, and a sixth death is handed a surviving NPC.
2. **Is "say the line, bank the Luck" load-bearing or a gimmick?** → **Kept,
   and flagged for an A/B desk playtest** (one run with it, one without). It is
   the only mechanism in the scenario that makes players *volunteer* for the
   genre instead of resisting it, which is why it stays until a playtest says
   otherwise.
3. **Does the table get told the mask's rule?** → **No — discoverable in Act
   Two**, via the 1954 clipping, with Hollis as the written fallback so it can
   never be missed. The alternative (putting it on the genre card) makes the
   whole game about nerve from minute one, and the discovery that **the answer
   is a name** is the best thing Act Two has.
4. **Does Denny speak?** → **No, until the mask is off, and then at length.**
5. **The slot's register.** → **Stale and now answered by the schedule**: the
   slate was rebuilt on 2026-10-25, the Berlin 1946 game was cut, and this
   moved to Saturday evening. It no longer has to be Thursday's relief valve.

### Still genuinely open — Tim's call, not the scenario's

**Whether the table is told at the start that the killer is an NPC** is settled
here (**yes** — the consent line above, because it removes a false worry
without spoiling anything). **But the related booking question is not a
scenario decision:** the advert no longer says a player character is involved,
and if the slate ever reverts to advertising it, the consent line and the
roster's Private-box variants both change together. The roster prints both
variants on every counsellor sheet so this can be flipped without a rewrite.

### New open questions, raised by writing v1

1. **Act One is ninety minutes of comedy and the GM has to carry it.** It is
   the hardest tonal ask on the slate and it is written as instructions rather
   than as material. If a playtest drags, Act One needs four written comic
   beats with actual dialogue, not a list of activities.
2. **"He waits" is doing a lot of work.** → **Closed at desk pass 1, and the
   answer was already in the Epilogue.** A table that refuses to approach for
   twenty minutes is not a failure state needing a chase — it is the
   **"Nobody said it"** row: whoever is left reaches Niamh's car, the camp
   comes down on schedule, and September 1986 is a bad year at Loon Point and
   it is the eighth one. That is a complete, honest, genre-correct ending and
   it is the one the evening has trained them toward. **Do not have him start
   walking.** If you want pressure that is not a chase, **he turns and walks
   toward the boathouse** — and the table works out for itself that if he is
   under it before Tuesday morning there is no ending at all. Cost without
   pursuit.
3. **Six counsellors and four second characters is ten sheets on one table.**
   Watch the physical logistics at a convention table that also has a map, a
   log, a Polaroid and a genre card on it.

## GM Notes (private)

- **Eleven initials, eight bad years.** The joist carries eleven sets of
  initials since 1931; the insurance log shows bad years in 1931, 1940, 1947,
  1954, 1962, 1971, 1979 and 1986 — eight. **Three wearers took it off before
  it cost anybody anything**, and those years look like any other year in the
  log. The arithmetic is deliberate, not a slip: *"every one of the eleven took
  it off eventually."* **If a player does the subtraction out loud — and the
  lore-digger will — that is the most hopeful fact in the scenario.** Let them
  have it.

- **Play Act One as a comedy and mean it.** The scenario does not work if the
  table is braced from minute one. They should be laughing when the body turns
  up, and the laugh should die in the room.
- **Denny is a nineteen-year-old who agreed.** Not possessed, not a monster,
  not a vessel. The mask made an argument and he found it persuasive, and that
  is the horror and it must survive to the end.
- **He waits.** When someone walks toward him and talks, he stands still. Do
  not have him flinch, raise the weapon, or tilt his head. He waits.
- **Do not pick a final girl.** Whoever survives, survives.
- **The mask is reasonable.** If a player ever puts it on, play the argument
  straight, in that character's own voice, using that character's own
  grievances — and make it a good argument, because it always is.
