# CLOSING WEEKEND — outline

**Call of Cthulhu 7e, modern rules · Camp Loon Point, upstate New York ·
Saturday 6 September 1986 · 6 players · 3h30**

---

## Overview

The season is over. The campers went home on Monday, the camp has been sold to a
development company, and on Tuesday the bulldozers come for the boathouse. Eight
counsellors stayed behind to close the cabins, drain the pipes, and have one last
night at the lake.

Six of them are the players. One of the other two has been wearing a mask since
June.

**Content warnings:** graphic slasher violence against young adults; sustained
stalking and pursuit; recreational drug and alcohol use; period attitudes left
in, not endorsed; the near-certainty that your first character dies. PCs are
18–22, not children — say so when you pitch it.

**Scaling:** written for **6**. At five, drop Russ; at four, drop Russ and
Bobbie and cut the Track B boathouse split. Nothing essential lives on one PC —
the three facts needed for Act Three (the mask's provenance, the 1931 pattern,
and Denny's absence from the party) are each reachable from two directions.

**Second characters are part of the design, not a consolation.** See *Dying
properly*, below.

---

## Design intent

1. **It is a slasher, played knowingly, and it is funny for ninety minutes
   before it stops being funny.** Act One is Nair in the shampoo, a stolen
   station wagon and a lake at sunset. The table should be laughing when the
   first body turns up. If Act One is played as horror, the whole thing is a
   grind and Thursday has three grinds in it.
2. **No elimination.** A player whose counsellor dies takes a second character
   inside two minutes, from a prepared set. Death costs you your plans, your
   gear and your friends — never your seat.
3. **The killer walks and the killer wins fights.** He is not a stat block to be
   solved with a deer rifle. There are no pursuit rolls: the GM places him, and
   he is always closer than the geometry says he should be. Players who engage
   him physically lose, and the scenario says so out loud on page one of the GM
   text so no table discovers it by surprise.
4. **The climax is a name, not a weapon.** The mask comes off when someone who
   knew the wearer before says his name to his face, close enough to be heard.
   Every instinct the genre has trained into the table says *run*. Act Three is
   six people deciding, in order, whether to walk toward it.
5. **The decision after that is the real one.** Once the mask is off there is a
   nineteen-year-old on the ground, two bodies in the lake, and a mask that
   needs a wearer. The scenario does not tell the table what to do with any of
   the three.

---

## Player experience

You get the whole last day: closing the cabins, siphoning the camp's gas,
arguing about who drives, the lake going gold at six and black at eight. You get
the bonfire, the beer, the dare, and the bit where two of you walk off into the
trees and only one walks back. Then the phone line is a cut cable rather than a
storm, the wagon won't turn over, and somebody is standing between cabin four
and the treeline not doing anything at all. By midnight you will have worked out
that this camp has had a bad year every seven to eleven years since 1931, that
the thing in the boathouse crawlspace is older than the camp, and that the
reason you can't find Denny is that you have been looking at him all night. Then
you have to decide which of you says his name.

---

## Plot summary — GM truth

**The mask is the scenario.** It is a crude thing: split plywood backed with
canvas, bound with wire, cut flat across the eyes, cheap as a prop and ugly in
the hand. Nobody made it for the camp — the camp was built on top of where it
was.

It does not possess. It *persuades*. Worn, it supplies a reason, and the reason
is always the same reason and it is always a good one: **the summer must not
end.** It makes the argument patiently, in the wearer's own voice, using the
wearer's own grievances, and the wearer agrees. It can be taken off. Every one
of the eleven people who have worn it took it off eventually, and then had to go
on living.

**Eleven wearers since 1931.** Loon Point's history is a line of bad years at
seven-to-eleven-year intervals — 1931, 1940, 1947, 1954, 1962, 1971, 1979 — each
written up locally as a drowning, a fire, a runaway, a bear. The camp office
keeps every one of them in the incident log because insurance requires it. The
log is a handout. Read in one sitting it is unmistakable.

The mask was burned in 1954 and again in 1971. It is in the boathouse
crawlspace, unburned. Whether what came back is the same object or a new one
made by the next wearer is a question the scenario deliberately leaves standing;
there is a 1972 receipt for plywood in the office files for the table that wants
to believe the duller answer.

**This year's wearer is Denny Vasquez**, nineteen, third summer on staff, the
quiet kind one everyone likes and nobody watches. He found it under the
boathouse in June, pulling out a dead raccoon. He has killed twice already:
Marcy Okonkwo in July (recorded as a drowning) and Tad Brisbane in August
(recorded as having walked off the job, which he had threatened to do all
summer, which is why it worked). Denny wrote both reports himself. He is good at
the paperwork.

**Tonight is different because the camp has been sold.** The mask's one argument
has stopped being a mood and become a deadline. There is no next summer to
protect, so the logic runs out to its end: if the summer cannot continue, then
nothing may leave the camp that would carry the end of it away.

**Denny is at the party until roughly 9pm**, visible, sober, helpful, the one who
fetches more wood. He is the one the party sends to check the fuse box.

## Dying properly

The thing that breaks slashers at a table is that a dead player is a bored
player for ninety minutes. So:

- **Nobody dies on one failed roll.** Death comes on the **second** failure
  inside a scene, and the GM narrates the first one as the near miss. Players
  see it coming, which is the genre.
- **Four prepared second characters**, kept face-down at the GM's elbow and
  handed over within two minutes: *Deputy Carol Prine*, 34, out here on a noise
  complaint that nobody made; *Hollis Yeardley*, 61, the camp cook, who has
  worked eight summers and read the log; *Niamh Stoll*, 24, Vanterra's
  surveyor, with the site plans and a car that works; *Peter Okonkwo*, 20, who
  has driven up from Albany to find out how exactly his sister drowned in four
  feet of water.
- Each second character arrives **knowing something the dead counsellor
  didn't**, so a death moves the investigation forward instead of stalling it.
  This is the engine of Act Two.
- The counsellor who survives to Act Three is whoever survives. The scenario
  does not pick a final girl and the GM must not either.

---

## Genre rules (one side of card, read aloud in the Introduction)

- **Say the line, bank the Luck.** Deliver a trope out loud and mean it —
  splitting up, "I'll be right back", the fake-out scare, the shower, the
  vehicle that won't start — and recover 1D10 Luck. Once per player per act.
  This exists to make players volunteer for the genre instead of resisting it.
- **He walks.** No chase rolls. He is placed, not raced.
- **Second failure kills.** First failure is the near miss.
- **He does not stay down.** Twice. On the third, something else is wrong.

## Structure

| Phase | Clock | Content | GM checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Introduction** | 0:00–0:15 | Safety line. Pregen taglines. Genre-rules card. Open at 4pm with the cabins half-closed. | Every player has said one line in character. |
| **Act One** — *the last day* | 0:15–1:15 | Closing the camp; the geography laid down by using it (cabins, mess, office, boathouse, the fuse box, the one road). Sunset. The bonfire. Two go into the trees; one comes back. **First body at ~1:05.** | The table has laughed; the map has been walked; the phone and the wagon are both already established as things that exist. |
| *break* | 10 min | On the body. | |
| **Act Two** — *the night* | 1:25–2:55 | Survival and investigation, splitting naturally into Track A (**the office** — incident log, the 1972 receipt, Marcy's and Tad's reports in Denny's handwriting) and Track B (**the boathouse** — the crawlspace, what else is down there, the burn marks). First counsellor deaths; second characters arrive carrying the facts. The road, the cable and the cars close off one at a time. | Before 2:55: the table knows the mask comes off by name, and knows the wearer is Denny. |
| **Act Three** — *the name* | 2:55–3:20 | Reaching him. No new information. Who walks forward, who holds the light, who can't. Then: a boy on the ground, two in the lake, a mask in someone's hands, and Niamh's car keys. | A decision was made out loud by a named player. |
| **Epilogue** | 3:20–3:35 | Per-survivor vignettes keyed to the ending. 8 September: the boathouse comes down. | |

**Hard rule:** at 2:45 on the clock, go to Act Three whatever is unfinished.

**Act Two pacing note:** if the clock has beaten the table, Hollis Yeardley
arrives (or speaks up, if already in play) and says the two things straight —
eight summers, he's read the log, and he knows what gets found under the
boathouse. He has no proof and the table need not believe him; he exists so that
Act Three is never unreachable.

### Endings (all four written)

1. **Said.** Someone says his name. The mask comes off. Then the real argument:
   what goes in Niamh's car, who tells Deputy Prine what, and what happens on
   Tuesday when a bulldozer puts a blade through the crawlspace.
2. **Burned.** Third time. The table will feel it has won and the epilogue will
   not contradict them. 1993 is not their problem.
3. **Taken.** Somebody drives it out. Whether it works away from the point is
   the one thing the scenario will not answer, in the epilogue or anywhere else.
4. **Worn.** A survivor puts it on, on purpose, with a reason they can state.
   Written as a real ending and played with respect, not as a punishment.

**If someone shoots:** Denny can be killed. He is nineteen and he is not
armoured. The mask comes off a body as easily as a face, and then it is in the
room with six people who have just shot their friend, and it is very reasonable,
and it has all night.

## Handouts

1. **The Loon Point incident log**, 1923–1986, insurance copy — the central
   prop. The pattern is visible without a roll if it is read as a whole.
2. **Two 1931 newspaper clippings** and one from 1954.
3. **Marcy's drowning report** and **Tad's separation notice** — same hand.
4. **The 1972 plywood receipt**, for the table that wants the duller answer.
5. **A Polaroid** of the 1986 staff, nine people, taken in June. Denny is at the
   edge of it holding something behind his leg.
6. **The camp map** — the centrepiece. Walked in Act One, fled through in Act
   Two. Needs to be good and needs to be on the table from minute fifteen.

## Open questions

1. **Second characters: four or six?** Four means a late death leaves a player
   waiting. Six is more prep and more sheets on a crowded table. Leaning four
   plus a stated rule that the GM can hand a player a surviving NPC.
2. **Is the "say the line, bank Luck" rule load-bearing or a gimmick?** Needs a
   desk playtest with it on and one with it off.
3. **The mask's rule — does the table get told?** Currently discoverable in Act
   Two (the 1954 clipping: the wearer's brother is recorded as having "called to
   him"). The alternative is to put it on the genre-rules card from the start
   and make the whole game about nerve rather than discovery. **Decide before
   writing Act Two.**
4. **Does Denny speak?** Leaning no, until the mask is off, and then at length.
5. **Thursday's register.** This slot used to be the day's relief valve. It now
   has to do that job through tone alone, between a 9am zombie game and Berlin
   1946. Watch it at the first playtest.
