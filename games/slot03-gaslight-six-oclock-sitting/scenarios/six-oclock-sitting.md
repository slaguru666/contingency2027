## GM ESSENTIALS (one glance, any GM)

- **System crash-course:** Call of Cthulhu 7e, Gaslight. Roll d100 under the
  skill; under half is a hard success, under a fifth extreme. 01–05 critical,
  96–00 fumble. A failed roll may be *pushed* by describing doing more of it —
  fail the push and the Keeper imposes a real consequence. Sanity: lose the
  points, then roll d100 against current Sanity; **over** means a temporary
  bout of madness.
- **Mechanics inline:** `[CoC: Skill — what it reveals or does]`
- **Two frightened strangers, no monster.** Nobody in this room is a threat.
  There is no combat and no creature to fight at any point.
- **The fraud is meant to be caught.** Every mechanism is findable. A table
  that catches all ten is the scenario *working*.
- **The clock counts play time** — pause it for the break.
- **Hard rule: at 2:45, go to Act Three regardless**, even mid-sitting. Bring
  the lamps up on a half-finished effect; that is a better scene anyway.
- **Sanity on offer: 2 at best, 7 at worst** — the three true details (0/1,
  1/1D2, 1/1D3) and Arthur's letter (0/1). A thorough table typically takes 3
  or 4. Do not add losses; this is a game about grief, not about damage.
- **Safety:** state the tool in use (X-card / open door) during seating, and
  **in the same breath offer the darkness as a choice** — see below. Thirty
  seconds, no ceremony. The bereavement material is the whole scenario and the
  exit ramp must be genuinely available.
- **The entity is never named.** Not in your narration, not in your notes, not
  if a player asks directly. See GM Notes.

## Overview

- **Event:** Contingency 2027 — Wednesday 27 January, Slot 3, evening,
  Searles Leisure Resort, Hunstanton. The last game of the convention's first
  day.
- **System:** Call of Cthulhu 7e — Cthulhu by Gaslight, **November 1894**
- **Setting:** A first-floor drawing room in Pimlico, London. One room, one
  evening, and fictional time running almost in step with the table's.
- **Tone:** Shabby, warm, humane, and then very quiet. A competent fraud
  exposed by professionals who are good at their jobs, in a room where
  something else is being patient.
- **Maximum Runtime:** 3 hours 30 minutes play + 10 minute break
- **Players:** 4–6. Six sheets exist; the game runs at four. At four the party
  is conjuror, physician, shorthand writer, journalist; the fifth seat is the
  believer and the sixth is the Society's photographer. **Drop order:
  photographer, then believer** — see the roster for what each costs.
- **Content warnings:** bereavement and its exploitation; a son killed on
  campaign in the Sudan, discussed at length; grief played straight and never
  for laughs; total darkness (optional, see below); and a scene in which a
  vulnerable person may be deceived by the players if they choose it. Safety
  tools in use.
- **Art Style:** Ink sketch / pencil illustration. Scenario direction: a
  respectable room with cheap secrets. Furniture, fabric, lamp-glow, hands.
  **The medium may be drawn; Mr Rée may not.**

### Design intent (read this first)

1. **One room.** No travel, no second location, no map. Everything the
   scenario needs is on the table or in the people around it.
2. **The fraud is complete and catchable.** Every effect has a mechanism,
   every mechanism is findable, and a thorough table will catch all of them.
   That is the scenario working, not the scenario being beaten — so run the
   **Trick Ledger** openly and let them score.
3. **The real thing is not hers.** The medium is a fraud who has recently
   started producing true details. She assumes her confederate has improved.
   He assumes she has. The actual source is a paying sitter, and he has no
   idea.
4. **No monster, no violence, no roll that ends it.** The climax is four
   people deciding what to do about two frightened strangers.
5. **Cheap on purpose.** Three handouts, no art budget required, no set-piece
   page. It is the Wednesday evening game at the end of a three-slot day and
   the slate needs one that costs almost nothing to build and almost nothing
   to run.

**Deviation from the house format, stated:** no separate set-piece. The
sitting *is* the set-piece and the Trick Ledger is its quick-reference.

### The darkness — offer it, never impose it

A materialisation sitting happens in near-dark under a red lamp, and actually
dimming the convention room is the single most effective thing you can do here.
It is also a thing some tables will dislike, and at a convention you cannot
know which you have.

**So put it in the safety line, as a question with a default of no:**

> *"This game is a séance, so there's an option: I can turn the lights in here
> right down for the middle section. It's good, and it is entirely optional —
> if anyone would rather not, we keep them up and nothing is lost. Shall I ask
> again when we get there?"*

Ask once at seating, confirm once at the top of Act Two, and if a single person
hesitates, keep the lights up. The scenario does not depend on it.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**Mrs Dorothea Veale**, 54, has been a materialising medium for eleven years
and has never once produced a genuine phenomenon. Her act is a mature,
competent fraud: a hinged cabinet, a telescoping reaching-rod, cheesecloth in a
false bustle, raps from a knee, and **Hobbs**, her son-in-law, under the floor
with the gas tap and a length of fishing line. She is not a monster. She is a
widow running a business, and she sleeps fine.

**Six months ago the act started being right.** Details she could not have
researched began arriving in her trance patter — names, regimental numbers, the
contents of unsent letters. Mrs Veale believes Hobbs has got ambitious and is
doing better research. Hobbs believes Mrs Veale has finally gone genuine and is
quietly terrified of her. **Neither has said a word**, because saying it aloud
would end the arrangement.

**The source is a sitter.** **Mr Edmund Rée**, 58, lost his son Arthur at Abu
Klea in January 1885 — twenty-one years old, and the body never came home.
Edmund had got the boy his commission through a friend at the Horse Guards, to
make a man of him, and against the boy's own wish. They parted badly. Since his
wife died in 1892, Edmund has attended **forty-one sittings** with nine
different mediums, always asking the same question, always in the same words,
always at the same hour. He has, by sheer unvarying repetition, worn a groove.
He has made a place for something to sit, and something has.

**It is not Arthur.** It is not quite pretending to be Arthur either — it
answers in Arthur's particulars because those are the particulars Rée brings,
the way water takes the shape of a jug. It is courteous, accurate, and
increasingly specific. What it is doing is **learning the room**: for six months
it has only been able to speak through whichever medium Rée was sitting with.
**Tonight is the forty-second sitting, and it has nearly enough.**

**The cruelty of the design:** Rée is innocent, bereaved, and the dangerous
object in the room. Mrs Veale is a criminal and almost irrelevant. The
investigators' professional duty — expose the medium, write it up, go home —
removes the only structure that has been containing this for half a year, and
nobody present will ever be able to explain why that was a mistake.

### Rée's question

He asks it at **three minutes past eight**, every time, in these words:

> *"Arthur. I should like to know whether you minded going."*

He has asked it forty-one times. He has never been answered.

**Tonight he is answered.** What answers him says: *"No, father. I was glad to
go, and I was glad you asked it of me."* That is a kindness, and it is almost
certainly a lie, and **nothing in this scenario will ever establish which** —
because the one document that could is Arthur's unsent letter, and the letter
says something else.

### Timeline

| When | What |
|---|---|
| Nov 1884 | Edmund secures Arthur's commission. They quarrel. Arthur sails. |
| 17 Jan 1885 | Arthur Rée killed at Abu Klea. He had written a letter home the night before and had not posted it. |
| Apr 1885 | The letter returns to Pimlico with his effects. **Edmund reads it once** and has never shown it to a living soul. |
| 1892 | Mrs Rée dies. Edmund attends his first sitting six weeks later. |
| May 1894 | Mrs Veale's act begins producing true details. Sitting twenty-two. |
| Sep 1894 | Hobbs tries to quit and is talked round. |
| Nov 1894 | Dr Pyne of the Society receives two complaints and commissions an exposure. |
| **Tonight** | Sitting forty-two. **Play begins at five o'clock.** |

### Revelations (GM checklist — tick them off)

1. Mrs Veale's phenomena are produced by mechanism. (The table will get this
   in Act One and should.)
2. There is a confederate, and he is under the floor.
3. The confederate is family — Hobbs is her son-in-law.
4. Mrs Veale will admit the fraud, fully, under moderate pressure.
5. **She will not admit the true details, because she cannot name them** — she
   thinks they are Hobbs's research.
6. **Hobbs thinks they are hers, and he is frightened of her.** Either of them
   alone looks like a liar; the two of them together prove neither is doing it.
7. Mr Rée has attended forty-one sittings with nine mediums.
8. He asks the same question, in the same words, at the same hour.
9. The true details are all drawn from *Rée's* life and losses — never from any
   other sitter's, and never from the investigators' until tonight.
10. One true detail quotes a document only Edmund Rée has ever read.
11. The phenomenon has only ever spoken through a medium **Rée was sitting
    with**. It follows him, not her.
12. **The third true detail is addressed to a player character** — which means
    it is no longer confined to Rée.

**Anything undelivered by the end of Act Two folds into Act Three**, where
Hobbs and Veale contradicting each other carries 5, 6 and 11 in two minutes.
Beyond that Act Three has **no new information** — only confirmation and
consequence.

## Player Experience

You have done eleven of these for the Society. You know where the reaching-rod
goes, you know what muslin looks like under a red lamp, you know the knee-rap
from the table-rap. Mrs Veale will not be difficult.

And she isn't. By seven o'clock you have the cabinet, the thread, the
confederate and the whole shabby machine. What you do not have is an
explanation for how a woman who has never left Pimlico knew the regimental
number, or the name of the dog, or exactly what was in the last letter — which
was never posted, and which only one man in this room has ever read.

He is sitting opposite you. He has been to forty-one of these in two years. He
is perfectly ordinary, perfectly polite, and at three minutes past eight he is
going to ask his question again.

## The Countdown (background clock)

There is no antagonist, so the clock is **the sitting's own timetable** — it
starts whether or not the investigators are ready, and Mrs Veale will not
delay it, because her sitters have paid.

| Clock | Fiction | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| 0:30 | 5:30 | The other sitters arrive together: Rée, Miss Carrow, and two who do not matter. Tea. This is the only chance to interview them unhurried |
| 1:10 | 5:55 | Mrs Veale asks everyone to take a seat, whether the search is finished or not |
| 1:15 | 6:00 | **The lamps go down.** Act Two begins on the hour and will not wait |
| 1:50 | 6:40 | Hobbs, under the floor, is cramped and getting careless. Every trick from here is one step easier to catch |
| 2:15 | 7:25 | Mrs Veale is tiring and goes to her stock patter — which is where the true details have been arriving |
| 2:35 | 8:03 | **Rée asks his question.** This happens on the fictional clock regardless of where the table is |
| 2:45 | 8:10 | Mrs Veale breaks trance badly, frightened, and calls for the lamps herself. **Act Three** |

**If the table stalls**, the lever is Mrs Veale's schedule: she has paying
sitters and a room booked and she moves things along, politely, without
noticing she is rescuing the pacing.

## Introduction — *A Clean Exposure by Friday* *(~15 minutes)*

### Slot Zero (before the fiction)

1. Seating. Safety line **and the darkness offer** in one breath (above).
2. Pitch each sheet in one line off the roster's essence lines.
3. Deal or choose. Five minutes maximum.
4. Open with the brief already in someone's hand.

### Opening (read aloud or paraphrase)

> Number eleven, Cambridge Street, Pimlico: a respectable door on a street of
> respectable doors, and a first-floor window with the curtains already drawn
> at five in the afternoon. Mrs Veale answers it herself, in grey, with flour
> on one wrist, and is so pleased to see you that for a moment it is difficult
> to remember you are here to destroy her.

### Player Briefing

- The **Society for Psychical Research** has had two complaints about Mrs
  Dorothea Veale, materialising medium, of Pimlico. Dr Pyne wants an exposure
  he can publish by Friday.
- The investigators have done **eleven** of these. They are good at it. They
  have a checklist (`H1-A`) and the Society's authority to search the room.
- Mrs Veale has agreed to the examination because refusing looks worse. She is
  being a good sport about it, which is unsettling.
- Tonight's sitting is at six. There will be four other sitters.
- **The brief's last line matters:** *the Society has no interest in the
  sitters.* Read it aloud. It will be wrong by half past eight.

### Atmosphere Notes

- **Warm, shabby, respectable.** A good fire, bad sherry, a cat. The room is
  trying hard.
- **Mrs Veale is likeable.** This is the most important instruction in the act.
  Play her as tired, funny, and courteous to everyone including the people
  dismantling her furniture.
- **Everything smells faintly of camphor and gas.**

### Hook into Act One

She shows them in, offers them the room, and says — entirely without irony —
*"Do look everywhere. I'd rather you did it now than wondered after."*

## ACT ONE — *Before* *(~60 minutes)*

**Shape:** arrival, tea, and a search. The investigators may examine everything
and should find most of it. Running in parallel: five minutes each with the
other sitters, which is where **Rée registers as a person rather than
furniture.** The act's real work is making the table like Mrs Veale slightly
more than they meant to.

### The room

A first-floor drawing room, eighteen feet by fourteen. A round table with a
baize cloth. Eight chairs. A **cabinet** in the corner — a curtained closet
on castors, the standard apparatus. A red glass lamp on a bracket. A
harmonium. A fire. Two windows, curtained and shuttered. A worn patch of
carpet by the cabinet, and under the carpet a section of floorboard that lifts.

### NPCs

**Mrs Dorothea Veale, 54** — *wants the rent paid and not to think about the
last six months.* Tired, shrewd, nearly kind. **Will confess the fraud under
moderate pressure** `[CoC: Persuade, Intimidate or Charm — one success is
enough; she has been waiting years for someone to make her stop]`. She will
**not** confess the other thing, because she cannot name it: pressed on the
true details, she says Hobbs has been doing better research, and she says it
with relief, and she is lying to herself in front of them.

**Hobbs, 31** — *wants out.* Under the floor from 5:45. Cramped, sweating,
and will talk to anyone who finds him. **The fastest route to Revelation 6:**
he thinks Mrs Veale has gone genuine and it has been frightening him for six
months. He has tried to quit twice.

**Mr Edmund Rée, 58** — *wants to be told he was forgiven.* Courteous,
precise, grey, dull. Talks about the weather and the trains. **He is the most
dangerous person in the building and must read as the least interesting person
in the room for the whole of Act One.** If a player asks why he comes, he says
*"habit, I suppose,"* and changes the subject competently.

**Miss Carrow, 60s** — *wants it all to be true.* The believer. Useful as a
witness, as a conscience, and as the person who says the unsayable thing in Act
Three. *(A PC at five players.)*

**Dr Pyne, Society secretary** — *wants a publishable exposure by Friday.* Not
present. `H1-B` is his entire appearance.

**Two sitters who do not matter** — a Mr Tasker and his sister. Give them one
trait each (he is deaf on the left; she takes notes she will never read) and
let them be furniture. They exist so that Rée is not conspicuously the only
sitter.

**Fallbacks:**
- **1–5:** `H1-A`, the Society's checklist, lists every one of these as a thing
  to look for. A table that works the checklist cannot miss the fraud.
- **6:** if nobody lifts the board, Hobbs's foot is audible at 1:50 when he
  cramps. The believer hears it and says so, loudly.
- **7, 9:** Mrs Veale volunteers both if asked anything at all about her
  regulars. She is proud of having regulars.
- **10:** if Miss Carrow is a PC, Mr Tasker's sister carries this line instead.

### The obvious professional moves — answer them

- **"We search her person."** The physician may, with her consent, which she
  gives wearily. Cheesecloth, a pencil, a shilling, and a photograph of a
  daughter. Give them the photograph; it is Hobbs's wife.
- **"We nail the floorboard down / stand on it."** Excellent, and it works.
  The sitting then produces **no** mechanical phenomena at all — and the three
  true details arrive anyway. This is the best possible version of Act Two and
  you should be delighted if they do it. Hobbs, trapped, is furious and silent.
- **"We refuse to sit and just publish."** Then Act Two happens *to* them in
  the hall: Mrs Veale sits without them, and Rée's question is asked through an
  open door, and the third detail still finds its player. Do not block this —
  move the scene, keep the beats.
- **"We interview the nine other mediums."** No time tonight, and the ledger
  names them. In the Epilogue, two of nine confirm Rée sat with them and that
  their own acts "came good" while he did.
- **"We bring in the police."** A constable can be fetched in twenty minutes
  and will take a fraud complaint. He will not take anything else, and his
  presence makes Act Three's exposure a prosecution. Let them; it costs Mrs
  Veale her liberty and changes nothing about Rée.

### Pacing Note — Act One

**Must be true before Act Two:** the cabinet and at least two other mechanisms
found; all four sitters spoken to; **Rée registered as a person.** If the
players have not spoken to Rée, have him speak to *them* — he asks the
shorthand writer, politely, whether notes are usually kept.

**Fast-track if past 1:05:** drop clues 8 and 10, let Mrs Veale confess the
fraud outright over tea (she will), and go to the table. The fraud is not the
scenario.

**Place the break here**, as the lamps go down.

## ACT TWO — *The Sitting* *(~90 minutes)*

**Shape:** lamps down, played long and slow. Run it as a **trick ledger** —
every mechanism the players catch is named aloud and scored, so the table feels
itself winning. Then a detail lands that is not on the ledger and cannot be put
there. Three such details, escalating. The third is addressed to a player
character.

### Confirm the darkness, then open

> *"Hands flat on the cloth, if you please, and the little fingers touching your
> neighbours'. You may break the circle whenever you like — I shan't be
> offended, and I shan't be able to stop you."*
>
> She says the last part as a joke. Everyone laughs. Then Hobbs takes the gas
> down from underneath and the room goes to the colour of a held hand over a
> candle, and the laughing stops on its own.

### THE TRICK LEDGER — GM quick-reference

**Keep this open and score it out loud.** When a player catches a mechanism,
name it, tick it, and say the count: *"That's four."* Use `H1-A` as the
scoresheet. Ten tricks, roughly in order of appearance.

| # | Effect | Mechanism | Caught by | Tell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Three raps for yes | Veale's knee against the table leg | `[CoC: Listen]`, or a hand under the table | The raps are always on her side |
| 2 | The table tilts | Her boot toe under the central pedestal | `[CoC: Spot Hidden]`, or anyone holding the table | It only tilts away from her |
| 3 | The lamp dims on command | Hobbs, gas tap below | **Automatic** if the floorboard is known | It dims a half-second *before* she asks |
| 4 | Cold draught across the circle | The shuttered window's sash cord, pulled from below | `[CoC: Natural World or Spot Hidden]` | The draught comes from the floor, not the window |
| 5 | A hand touches a sitter's neck | The blacked reaching-rod from inside the cabinet | `[CoC: Spot Hidden]`; the conjuror automatically | The touch is always on the shoulder away from the cabinet |
| 6 | The harmonium plays four notes | Fishing line, cabinet to keyboard | `[CoC: Listen — hard success]`, or grabbing the line | Always the same four notes |
| 7 | Ectoplasm at the cabinet's edge | Cheesecloth, bustle, reaching-rod | **Automatic** under any light; `[CoC: Spot Hidden]` in the dark | It smells of starch |
| 8 | A child's voice from the corner | Veale, ventriloquial, mouth closed | `[CoC: Listen or Art/Craft (Acting)]` | Her throat moves. Watch her throat |
| 9 | A sitter's ring is removed and returned | Sleight of hand, done during the circle-joining | `[CoC: Sleight of Hand]` to spot; **automatic** for the conjuror | She touched that hand twice |
| 10 | A written message on a blank slate | Prepared slate, swapped in the dark | `[CoC: Spot Hidden]`, or examining both slates | The chalk is dry and the writing is old |

**Scoring guidance:** at **six or more** caught, Mrs Veale knows she is beaten
and starts going through the motions — say that her patter gets mechanical,
because it is about to matter that her patter is where the true things come
from. At **nine or ten**, she stops performing entirely at about 7:20 and sits
there in the dark being quietly dismantled, and *the details keep coming
anyway.*

**If the floorboard was nailed down in Act One**, tricks 3, 4 and 6 simply do
not happen. Say so. The absence is better than the effect.

### The three true details

These are the scenario. They arrive **inside her ordinary patter**, in her
ordinary voice, with no effect attached and no theatre around them — that is
why they land. Do not foreshadow them with a sound cue. **No roll detects
them**; `[CoC: no roll resolves these. They are simply said, and they are
simply true.]`

**DETAIL ONE — the regimental number.** *(~6:50, after trick 4 or 5)*

> *"— and there's a young man here, and he's very warm about it, he says the
> number, he says one-nine-seven-four-one, he says you'll know the number —"*

It is Arthur Rée's service number. **Mr Rée has never spoken it in this house.**
Mrs Veale has no access to it; a `[CoC: Library Use]` check later confirms
service numbers of the Sudan campaign were not published.

Rée says, quietly, *"Thank you."* Nothing else. **Sanity 0/1** for any
investigator who understands what just happened.

---

**DETAIL TWO — the letter.** *(~7:30, after the ledger is mostly full)*

> *"He says — this is a funny thing to say — he says he never posted it, and
> he's sorry about the second page."*

**The second page.** Arthur's unsent letter has two pages, and Edmund Rée is
the only living person who has read it. Not the Society, not a medium, not his
late wife. **Nobody in the room can account for this.**

Mrs Veale, in trance, says it the way she says everything. Hobbs, under the
floor, hears it and stops breathing — if anyone is down there with him, they
feel him go rigid. **Sanity 1/1D2.**

Rée asks, for the first time in forty-two sittings, a second question:
*"Which second page?"* And the medium's patter rolls straight past it, because
**the thing answering is not having a conversation. It is practising.**

to the private-grief table at its head, pick **one** player character — by
preference the most sceptical player at the table, not the most invested — and
have the medium name **their** particular. The named date. The kept object. The
thing not said at a bedside. Whatever the sheet carries.

Deliver it exactly as the first two: flat, mid-patter, no theatre, and then
carry on talking about something else.

> *"— and there's another one here, standing a bit back, and this one's not for
> the gentleman, this one's for you, dear —"*

Then the particular. Then: *"— she says it wasn't your fault, and she says you
know that it was."*

**Sanity 1/1D3** for the named investigator; **0/1** for everyone who hears it
and knows the sheet.

**Why this beat exists:** up to now the phenomenon has only ever spoken to Rée,
through whichever medium Rée sat with. Revelation 11 says it follows him. **This
is the moment that stops being true.** It has learned the room, and the room now
contains six other people. Do not explain that at the table. Let the player who
was named explain it, in Act Three, to the others.

**If the roster's grief table is thin or a player has opted out of personal
material via the safety tool**, use this instead, addressed to the whole table:
*"— and this one's not for the gentleman at all, this one's for the lot of you,
and it's only this: she says, when you write it up, do be accurate."*
That is a colder beat and an acceptable substitute.

### Rée's question — 8:03 on the fictional clock

This happens **whatever the table is doing**. If they are arguing, if the lamps
are up, if Hobbs is being hauled out of the floor by his collar — Edmund Rée
waits for a gap, and he takes it, and he says:

> *"Arthur. I should like to know whether you minded going."*

Forty-one times unanswered. Tonight, through Mrs Veale's exhausted, beaten,
mechanical patter, in the voice she uses for everything:

> *"No, father. I was glad to go, and I was glad you asked it of me."*

**Hold the table on that.** Do not move, do not narrate, do not cut away. Mrs
Veale comes out of trance badly about fifteen seconds later, frightened, and
calls for the lamps herself — which is the first genuinely unplanned thing she
has done in eleven years.

### Pacing Note — Act Two

**Must be true before Act Three:** the fraud is proven; **all three details
delivered**; Rée's question asked and answered.

**Cuts, in this order:**
1. Drop tricks 6, 9 and 10. Seven caught mechanisms feel like ten.
2. Merge details one and two into a single speech — the number *and* the second
   page in one breath at about 7:20. It is less patient and it works.
3. **Never cut detail three.** It is the reason the scenario is not just a
   well-made debunking. If you are desperately short, cut straight from detail
   one to detail three and let Rée's question follow immediately.

**At 2:45 bring the lamps up wherever you are**, mid-effect if necessary.

## ACT THREE — *After* *(~30 minutes)*

**A decision, not a die roll.** `[CoC: no roll resolves Act Three.]`

### Opening (read aloud)

> The gas comes up and the room is just a room again: a baize cloth, eight
> chairs, a curtain on castors and a woman of fifty-four with her hair come
> down, sitting very still with her hands in her lap. Somebody's sherry has gone
> over. Under the floor, something shifts its weight. And Mr Rée is putting on
> his gloves, carefully, one finger at a time, because he has what he came for
> and he does not want to be here while anyone discusses it.

### What the room now contains

- **Mrs Veale**, exposed, and she knows it, and she is more frightened of the
  last six months than of the Society.
- **Hobbs**, who will come up out of the floor if called and will say — in
  front of her, which is the scene — *"I thought it was her. All this time I
  thought it was her."*
- **Mr Rée**, who has been answered, and who would like to go home.
- **Miss Carrow**, who says the unsayable thing: *"But some of it was true.
  You all heard. Some of it was true."*
- **A player character who was named**, and has to decide whether to say so.

**Staging note:** get Veale and Hobbs in the same room contradicting each other
before any decision is made. Each alone looks like a liar. The two of them
together prove neither is doing it, and that is the fact the decision has to be
made on. If the players have not found Hobbs, Mrs Veale calls him up herself.

### Arthur's letter

**Only if a player asks Rée directly about the second page** — not otherwise —
he takes it out. He has carried it for nine years. **Sanity 0/1** for any
investigator who reads it beside what the dark said.

**Hand over `H3-A`.** Do not read it aloud.

> **`H3-A` — Arthur Rée to his father, 16 January 1885, not posted**
>
> *Father,*
>
> *We go up to the wells tomorrow and there is a chance this reaches you late,
> so I shall say it badly rather than not at all.*
>
> *I did mind. I minded it very much, and I mind it still, and I have not been
> able to write this page four times now for fear of how it would look beside
> the first one, which is all about the horses and is quite cheerful and is
> perfectly true as well.*
>
> *I do not think you were wrong. I think you were in a hurry, and I think you
> wanted it settled, and I think if either of us had said one more sentence in
> the hall that morning we should have got there. I have had five months of
> composing that sentence and I cannot find it, so here is a worse one: I am
> not angry and I am not reconciled, and I would rather you knew both.*
>
> *Tell mother the first page only.*
>
> *Your son,*
> *Arthur*

**The point of the handout:** the thing in the dark said *"I was glad to go."*
The letter says *"I did mind."* **The entity gave Edmund Rée the kinder answer
and it was not the true one** — and the players are now holding the proof, in
front of a man who has waited nine years and is finally at peace.

Whether they show it to him is the single hardest choice in the scenario and it
is not on the choices table below, because it is not about the case. If they do
show him: he reads it, and folds it, and says *"Yes. That's his hand."* And
thanks them. And that is all, and it is terrible.

### The Choices (present plainly, then get out of the way)

| Choice | What it does | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Expose Mrs Veale and publish** | The brief is discharged. Dr Pyne gets his exposure by Friday, and it is a good one | Mrs Veale is finished and possibly prosecuted. **Rée goes elsewhere.** The forty-third sitting happens in a month in a room where nobody competent is watching |
| **Expose Rée** | Name him in the report as the source | On evidence no coroner would accept, against a man who has done nothing but grieve in a regular pattern. The Society will not print it. Pyne will ask them to reconsider their methods |
| **Tell nobody, and sit again next week with the investigators present** | Keeps the structure that has contained this for six months, and puts trained observers in the room | A lie to the Society, a fraud left operating, and a standing weekly appointment with the thing in the dark. **And it works.** This is the responsible answer and it is professionally ruinous |
| **Finish tonight's sitting properly** | Lamps down again, no tricks, Hobbs out of the floor, and let it say what it came to say | Nobody knows what it came to say. It has been learning the room for six months and tonight it addressed someone who was not Rée |

**Compromise endings players will invent — all legitimate:**
- **Publish the fraud, suppress the details.** The most likely outcome and a
  genuinely defensible one. The report is accurate and incomplete, and the
  shorthand writer has to decide what goes in the transcript.
- **Pay Mrs Veale to stop.** She would take it. It does not touch Rée.
- **Take Rée's sittings over themselves** — the Society as his medium, monthly,
  documented. Clever, and it makes the investigators the new cabinet.
- **Tell Rée the truth about the fraud and let him choose.** He listens
  carefully, thanks them, and says *"I think I shall go on going, if it's all
  the same to you."* There is nothing to be done with that.
- **Burn the letter.** Someone will suggest it. It is not theirs to burn.

### If someone shoots

There is nothing to shoot. The chaos contingency is **a scene**: a player
seizes Mrs Veale, or breaks the cabinet, or hauls Hobbs up by the throat, or
tells Rée his son is in hell. Let it happen, let Miss Carrow start crying, and
let the constable arrive if one was fetched. Then: **Mrs Veale presses charges
for assault, the Society disowns the report, and nothing whatever has changed
about Mr Rée.** That is the Pyrrhic outcome and it is written below.

### Pacing Note — Act Three

**Must be true before the Epilogue:** the decision said out loud, in front of
both Veale and Rée. Make a player state it to their faces; do not accept a
decision taken in the hall.

**If you are behind:** cut the letter (a real loss — it is the scenario's
proof) and go straight to the choices. If you are at 3:10, cut the choices
table discussion and ask the table one question: *"What does the report say?"*
That is the same decision in one sentence.

**No new information.** Everything in this act is confirmation.

## Epilogue — *What the Society Published* *(~15 minutes)*

### Outcomes

| Outcome | Condition | What it reads like |
|---|---|---|
| **Clean exposure** | Veale published, details suppressed or omitted | *Proceedings*, vol. XI: a model exposure, ten mechanisms, named and diagrammed. It is cited for thirty years. Mrs Veale takes in sewing. **In March, in Camberwell, a medium of no previous ability begins producing true details**, and a gentleman of about sixty has started attending |
| **Containment** | They sit again, keep observing, tell the Society nothing | Eleven more sittings, documented in a hand that gets steadier. The thing is patient and courteous and answers questions now. By the eighth sitting it has stopped using Arthur's particulars, and by the eleventh it is using one of theirs |
| **Exposure of Rée** | He is named as the source | The Society declines to publish. A copy circulates privately. Rée reads it, in a reading room, by accident, in 1896 |
| **Pyrrhic** | Violence, or the letter shown and the sitting abandoned | Mrs Veale prosecutes or is prosecuted; the report is disowned; Mr Rée goes home with the true answer instead of the kind one, and does not attend a forty-third sitting, or anything else |

### Per-PC vignettes (keyed to outcome)

One each, second person, three sentences.

- **The conjuror.** He caught every trick in the room, named them in order, and
  was right about all ten. He has started doing a thing with his hands when he
  cannot sleep: counting them off, and getting to ten, and starting again.
- **The physician.** He examined her person, her pulse and her pupils and found
  a tired woman of fifty-four in good health. He has written to two colleagues
  about the regimental number and neither has replied.
- **The shorthand writer.** The transcript is verbatim and complete, which was
  the job. **Whether it was filed complete is the only decision that will
  outlive any of them**, and it was theirs.
- **The journalist.** Four columns, a diagram of the cabinet, and a byline. It
  is the best thing he has written. Nothing in it is untrue and he knows
  exactly how much is missing.
- **The believer** *(if played)*. She was right, and nobody will ever say so,
  and being right has not been what she expected it to be.
- **The photographer** *(if played)*. Eleven plates. Ten show a curtain, a
  rod, a woman's wrist and a quantity of cheesecloth — a clean exposure, all
  of it. **Plate seven is the one he develops last**, and it shows the circle
  in the dark, and it shows one more pair of hands flat on the cloth than there
  were people in that room, and the hands are not nearly finished.

### Table debrief

One question, then stop: **"The thing in the dark gave him the kinder
answer. Was that a mercy?"** Do not answer it.

### Closing Scene (read aloud, adapt to outcome)

> Cambridge Street at half past eight on a November evening: wet railings, a
> smell of coal, a cab going up towards Victoria with one lamp out. Behind one
> first-floor window the gas is full up and a woman is putting eight chairs
> back against the wall, the way she does every Wednesday. And a grey, polite,
> entirely ordinary man walks down to the corner with his hat in his hand,
> because he has been told what he came to be told, and it has made him so
> happy that he has forgotten to put it on.

## The Tangent

*A bonus scene for a table that finishes early.*

**The ninth medium.** Mrs Veale's ledger names her competitors, and one of
them — **Mr Alfred Seddon**, of Hoxton, "trumpet and direct voice" — is
twenty minutes away and still awake, and will see them for a shilling.

Seddon is a fraud of a much lower order and admits it in forty seconds, because
he is drunk and lonely and has been wanting to tell someone. Rée sat with him
nine times in 1893. Seddon stopped doing sittings in April. Asked why, he says
his act started being right, and he did not care for it, and he now sells
gramophones.

He also says — and this is the Tangent's whole purpose — that he was not the
first. That there was an old woman in Deptford before him who said the same
thing. And he does not know whether she stopped.

Let the players work out that the groove is nine mediums deep and has been
getting better. Then call time.

## Open questions — **resolved for v1**

1. **How literally to run the darkness?** → **Offered, never imposed, and
   named in the safety line** with a default of no. Asked once at seating and
   confirmed once at the top of Act Two; one person's hesitation keeps the
   lights up. The scenario does not depend on it.
2. **The third true detail is addressed to a PC, so it must come from the
   sheets.** → Resolved by requiring a **private-grief table at the head of the
   roster file**: per PC, one named person, one date, one physical particular
   never said aloud. Act Two points at that table rather than carrying its own
   list, and a written substitute covers a thin table or a player who has opted
   out of personal material.
3. **Does the entity get a name in the GM text?** → **No.** It is a shape grief
   wore. Naming it makes it a monster and gives the players something to
   research, and there is nothing to research.
4. **What is the sixth seat?** → **The Society's photographer** — plates,
   tripod, magnesium flash. Period-true (spirit photography was a live SPR
   concern), a lane nothing else on the roster covers, and evidence that
   outlives the table's decision, which is what gives the Epilogue its last
   image. Drop order: photographer first, then the believer.

### New open questions, raised by writing v1

1. **Does the flash fire in Act Two?** The photographer's magnesium flash is
   the one piece of hard theatre available in a dark room, and v1 deliberately
   does *not* schedule it — the three true details are quiet by design and a
   flash competes with them. Current intent: the player chooses when to fire
   it, once, and whatever it catches is on a plate nobody sees until the
   Epilogue. **Untested, and it may need a written beat.**
2. **Is Mrs Veale too likeable to expose?** Act One is instructed to make the
   table like her. If a playtest shows the decision becoming obvious — nobody
   wants to ruin her — she needs one unlikeable beat: she has done this to a
   bereaved mother and it is in the ledger.
3. **Six in a circle of eight.** With six investigators, the circle is almost
   all professionals and the sitters stop being people. Watch whether Rée
   disappears at six players, because if he does, the scenario has no subject.

## GM Notes (private)

- **Play Mrs Veale as a working woman, not a villain.** The scenario's whole
  structure depends on the exposure being easy and feeling slightly unkind.
- **Mr Rée must be boring for sixty minutes.** Resist every instinct to make
  him interesting in Act One. He talks about trains. The horror is that the
  dangerous object in the room is the one person nobody wrote anything down
  about.
- **The entity is never named, never described, never seen, and never
  explained.** It is courteous and it is accurate and it is practising. If a
  player asks what it is, the honest answer in the fiction is that nobody in
  1894 has a word for it, and the honest answer at the table is that the
  scenario does not know either.
- **Do not let the dice resolve anything that matters.** Three separate beats
  in this scenario are explicitly no-roll. Say so out loud when you get to
  them; a convention table reaches for dice when it is nervous, and this game
  wants them nervous with their hands flat on the cloth.
- **The kinder answer was a lie.** That is the scenario. Everything else is
  furniture.
