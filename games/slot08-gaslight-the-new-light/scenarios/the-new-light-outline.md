# [Contingency 2027 — Slot 8] THE NEW LIGHT

> *The first house in the county with electric light. The dark had to go somewhere.*

**Status:** outline (pre-v1).

---

## Overview

- **Slot:** Friday 29 January 2027, afternoon
- **System:** Call of Cthulhu 7e — Cthulhu by Gaslight, November 1895
- **Setting:** **Harlow Mote**, a manor on the Welsh border, wired throughout for
  electric light in 1894 and lit by its own turbine in the old mill house. One
  building, one night, rain.
- **Players:** 5, pregenerated — sent down by an insurance office after the
  consulting engineer who installed the system died in the house: an assessor, an
  electrical engineer, a physician, a solicitor's clerk, and a folklorist the
  office does not know it is paying for. Runs at 4 (drop the clerk).
- **Runtime:** 3h30 play in a 4-hour slot
- **Content warnings:** drowned children (historical, discussed in records and
  never depicted); class exploitation; confinement in total darkness; a bargain
  that requires a living person to be given up to it; an elderly character's
  death is a likely outcome.

## Design intent

1. **Not a descent.** The convention already has one flooded hole in it on
   Thursday night. This scenario is indoors, above ground, and lit — which is the
   whole idea.
2. **The horror is maintenance.** Nothing here was summoned. Something was being
   *kept*, quietly, as a household chore, by people who were never told why, and
   the chore has become impossible.
3. **The servant is the authority.** The person who understands this house is the
   housekeeper, and she was born in it when it was hers. The gentry in the drawing
   room know nothing and will be no help, which is the period doing the work.
4. **The climax is a signature.** Not a banishing, not a fight. Someone has to
   agree to a thing, in a book, in their own name, and the investigators are the
   only people present who understand what that means.
5. **Every light switch is a decision.** Give the players control of the house's
   lighting from Act One and let them learn what turning things on costs.

## Player experience

Sir Hubert Ames made his money in dynamos and has spent a great deal of it making
Harlow Mote the brightest house in three counties — two hundred and twelve lamps,
a turbine in the old mill, and not a candle on the premises. The insurance office
would not normally care, except that the engineer who commissioned the system was
found in the long gallery in September with his pocket-book in his hand and no
mark on him at all.

The family are charming and know nothing. The servants are not charming and know a
great deal, and the housekeeper, who was born here when the house still belonged
to her family, has spent eighteen months doing a job she cannot describe and has
now lost. The leat that used to run round the east lawn was re-cut in '93 to feed
the turbine. The parish register says three children drowned in that leat between
1680 and 1700, and then none — for two hundred years — until the gardener's boy
last month.

It is raining. The water is rising. Every lamp in the house is burning, and that
is the problem.

## Plot summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**The arrangement.** In 1698 the Garnocks, who held Harlow Mote for four hundred
years, came to an accommodation with what lives in the water under the east lawn.
The terms are recorded in the **household book** — not a grimoire, a
*housekeeping ledger*, kept in the still-room with the linen inventories and the
candle accounts, because that is what it is. The terms are three:

1. One room of the house is kept dark. Always. The door is shut, the shutters are
   pinned, no light is carried in.
2. The leat runs its old course, round and back, and is never straightened.
3. A name is written in the book, and that person is the one who keeps the room.

In exchange: the land drains, the mill never floods, and nobody drowns. The three
children in the register are from *before* the arrangement. After 1698 there is
not one drowning in two hundred years, in a parish with a leat running through it.

**What broke it.** Sir Hubert bought the house in 1891 from the last impoverished
Garnocks. In 1893 he re-cut the leat straight to drive the turbine. In 1894 he lit
the house — including, eventually, the dark room, which his electrician wired
because it was on the plan and nobody told him not to.

**Mrs Jane Garnock**, 68, the last of her line, stayed on as housekeeper in the
house she was born in. Her name is the one in the book. For eighteen months she
has been keeping the arrangement alone and by hand: unscrewing a bulb, pinning a
shutter, turning a switch back off, inventing reasons. She has not told anyone,
because telling Sir Hubert would mean explaining, and explaining would mean being
dismissed as mad by a man who owns her birthplace.

She is losing. The house is too bright and the leat is too straight. The gardener's
boy drowned in October in eleven inches of water. **Wilmot Hale**, the consulting
engineer, worked out roughly what was happening from the lighting loads and the
household book, went to the long gallery at night to test it, and died there.

**The thing itself** is never seen and never named. It is a tenancy, not a
predator. It has no grievance and no malice; it had an agreement and the agreement
has been broken by people who did not know it existed. It is doing the only thing
it has ever done, which is take what it was promised.

**The climax.** The arrangement can be restored. It needs the leat turned back
(days of work, and the turbine dead, and Sir Hubert's patent with it), the room
dark again, and **a name in the book**. Jane Garnock is dying — her heart, and she
knows it — and the line ends with her. A new name has to go in, and it cannot be
a dead one, and it cannot be written by someone who does not understand the terms.
Which, by Act Three, is nobody in the house except the five people the insurance
office sent.

## Structure

| Section | Budget | Cumulative | GM checkpoint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduction | 15 min | 0:15 | Pregens dealt, safety line, the office's brief read, arrival in rain |
| Act One — The House | 60 min | 1:15 | Hale's death scene examined; the lighting plan understood; Mrs Garnock met and not believed |
| *break* | 10 min | — | Ends on the lights failing once, briefly, everywhere |
| Act Two — The Book | 90 min | 2:45 | The household book found; the burial register read; the leat and the turbine connected; the dark room identified |
| Act Three — The Name | 30 min | 3:15 | The terms understood; the book open; the decision made |
| Epilogue | 15 min | 3:30 | Per-PC vignettes; what the office was told |

- **Act One — The House.** Arrival, the family at dinner, the long gallery, the
  body's circumstances, and two hundred and twelve lamps. Establish early that the
  players can switch lights on and off, and that the servants react to it.
- **Act Two — The Book.** The still-room, the parish chest, the mill house and
  the turbine, and Mrs Garnock — who will not explain until a player gives her a
  reason to think they will not have her committed. **Splits 3+2:** the house
  track (book, servants, dark room) and the ground track (leat, mill, register).
  Each holds half of the terms.
- **Act Three — The Name.** A decision, not a die roll, and every option is
  written. Restore it and find a name — Jane's is no good, Sir Hubert will not
  sign what he does not believe, and a player character can. Refuse, and leave
  with a true and useless report for the insurance office. Break it outright —
  drain the leat entirely, which is possible, costly, and nobody in the scenario
  knows what it releases. Or sign a name that is not the signer's own, which is
  the ugliest option and the one tables will invent.
- **Set-piece:** the house going dark, floor by floor, as the turbine is shut
  down by someone — and the players deciding which rooms get the remaining lamps.
  Own GM quick-reference page: a lighting board with twelve switches.

## Cast

- **Mrs Jane Garnock**, 68, housekeeper — *wants to hand it on before her heart
  stops.* Born in this house when it was hers. The only competent person in it.
  Will not volunteer anything to anyone who talks down to her.
- **Sir Hubert Ames**, 57 — *wants the turbine patent to carry.* Generous, loud,
  entirely sincere, and constitutionally unable to believe any of this. Not a
  villain; an obstacle with a chequebook.
- **Lady Ames** — *wants to know why the staff keep resigning.* Closer to the
  truth than her husband and has told herself it is nerves.
- **Wilmot Hale**, deceased — *wanted to be thorough.* His pocket-book is the
  scenario's central handout and it stops mid-sentence.
- **Tunnicliffe**, the gardener — *wants someone punished for the boy.* The
  scenario's grief, and the reason the clock has a floor under it.

## Open questions

1. Is the dark room's location fixed, or does the house's plan disagree with
   itself? A plan that will not add up is a lovely Act Two beat but costs a
   handout and a lot of GM text.
2. Can a player character actually sign and survive the epilogue? Current intent:
   **yes** — it is a life sentence, not a death sentence, and the epilogue plays
   it as forty quiet years. Confirm, because it changes the whole weight of Act Three.
3. How much does Sir Hubert need to witness? A climax he never understands is
   more period-true; a climax he refuses in front of the players is more dramatic.
4. Gaslight pregens: does the folklorist know about arrangements of this kind
   generally, giving the table one person with a frame? Current lean: yes, but
   with the wrong county's version, so the detail still has to be earned.
