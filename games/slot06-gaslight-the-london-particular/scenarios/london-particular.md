### Design intent (read this first)

1. **Mobile, and deliberately so.** The convention has three other Gaslight games
   and every one of them is a single room or a single building. This one is the
   city: hansoms, the river, rooftops, four districts in a night. If it ever
   settles into one location for forty minutes, it has gone wrong.
2. **The rule is findable and the rule is the game.** It travels in thick air and
   it is stranded in clear air. Once the players have that, the scenario stops
   being a mystery and becomes a calculation — *where will it be when the fog
   lifts?* — which is a far better climax than a chase.
3. **Opens on a body, pitches in one line.** It is the first game of the
   convention at nine in the morning. No briefing scene, no hiring, no exposition.
4. **It has no malice.** It drifts. The deaths are incidental, which is worse
   than if they were not.
5. **The human antagonist is sympathetic and wrong.** Hollis has been containing
   this for three years and has saved more lives than he has cost. The scenario
   never lets the players feel clean about what they do to him.

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**Hollis & Son**, a coal-tar and aniline works on the River Lea, ran a new
process in the autumn of 1893. What came off the retorts that November was not in
any book and has never been repeated. **Benjamin Hollis**, then 51, lost two men
in a night and spent the following week discovering three things:

1. It is diffuse — closer to a weather than an animal. There is nothing to shoot.
2. It can only hold together and move where the air is thick enough to carry it.
   Fog, smoke, steam. In clear air it disperses to something inert and
   **stranded**: it settles where it stood and waits, for days or months, until
   the air comes back.
3. It can be **steered**, crudely, by someone who controls a large chimney. Fire
   the stacks and the air thickens; bank them and it thins. Hollis has been doing
   exactly that for three years, pushing it out over the marshes and the river
   and away from streets with people in them, writing off the occasional death as
   bronchitis in a bad winter.

He has, by any honest reckoning, saved a great many lives. He has also never told
anyone, because the works employs five hundred and twelve men and the day this
becomes known is the day it closes.

**Why it is going wrong now.** Hollis is 54 and ill. His son **Edward**, 26, has
been running the works since September, has doubled the output, and fires the
stacks on a production schedule rather than a weather one. He does not know what
he is steering. He thinks his father's rules about the chimneys are an old man's
superstition. Four deaths in four weeks is the result.

**Tonight.** The forecast fog is general and will not lift until first light. The
thing will have the whole basin to drift through and will go wherever the
thickest air takes it, which — because Edward has the stacks running for a
Thursday delivery — is *inward*, up the river, towards the City.

**The window.** When the fog lifts it strands. In clear air, for perhaps six
hours, it is a locatable, inert, vulnerable thing lying in whatever yard or
alley or stretch of water it was over at dawn. The players can calculate where
that will be from the Met Office readings, the wind, and the stacks' firing
schedule. Getting that sum right is the scenario.

**Dealing with it** needs the works. The only thing that touched it in 1893 was a
reagent off Hollis's own process, and he has three carboys of it and the only
copy of the method.

## Player Experience

Four people have died in four weeks, each on a night of thick fog, each found in
the open street with no wound on them and their lungs full of something that is
not soot. There is nothing to connect the four — not district, not trade, not
hour. Mary Pargeter died in Bow. A lighterman died off Wapping Stairs. A boy of
nine died in Limehouse Causeway. A clerk died on Blackfriars Bridge.

The Meteorological Office keeps a fog register, and the fog register connects
them perfectly. Each of them died exactly where the fog lay thickest that night.

Tonight's forecast is the worst of the winter. By eight it will be from the
marshes to Westminster and it will not lift before dawn — and whatever moves in
it will have the whole of London to move through, and at first light it will stop
wherever it happens to be. For a few hours in clear air it can be found. Then you
wait for the next fog, and the next four.

## Open questions (carried from the outline — resolve or delete)

1. **How hard is the Act Three sum?** It must be solvable by a table that has
   paid attention and must not become homework. Current intent: three inputs
   (fog register, wind, stack schedule), a stated rule of thumb from Benjamin, and
   a GM fallback where Benjamin simply tells them if they are stuck at 2:40.
2. Does the thing get a name or an explanation? Current intent: neither. It came
   off a retort in 1893 and the scenario never says what it is.
3. A nine-year-old among the four dead — in the backstory, never on screen.
   Confirm that is the right weight for the convention's opening game, or make the
   fourth victim an adult.
4. The Limehouse chase needs to be frightening without anything visible. That is
   a hard scene to write and it is the reason this game needs two playtests, not
   one.
5. Four Gaslight games now share a period. Build their handout furniture —
   letterheads, registers, ledger rules, type — once, together.
