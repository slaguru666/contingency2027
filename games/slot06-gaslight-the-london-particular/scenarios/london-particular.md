## GM ESSENTIALS (one glance, any GM)

- **System crash-course:** Call of Cthulhu 7e, Gaslight. d100 under the skill;
  under half is a hard success, under a fifth extreme. **01 is a critical. 100
  is always a fumble; if the skill is under 50, 96–00 fumbles.** Push a failed
  roll by doing more of it — fail the push and the Keeper
  imposes a real consequence. Sanity: lose the points, then roll d100 against
  current Sanity; **over** means a temporary bout of madness.
- **Mechanics inline:** `[CoC: Skill — what it reveals or does]`
- **Nothing can be shot.** It is diffuse. There is no combat in this scenario
  and no round in which a weapon is the answer.
- **The clock counts play time** — pause it for the break.

## Overview

- **Event:** Contingency 2027 — Thursday 28 January, Slot 6, **evening**,
  Searles Leisure Resort, Hunstanton.
- **System:** Call of Cthulhu 7e — Cthulhu by Gaslight, **December 1896**
- **Setting:** London, in the worst fog winter since '79. Limehouse, Bow, the
  Lea marshes, the river, and whichever districts the fog takes next.
- **Tone:** Cold, wet, mobile, procedural. A city-wide hunt for a weather.
- **Maximum Runtime:** 3 hours 30 minutes play + 10 minute break
- **Players:** 4–6. Six sheets; runs at four. Drop the cabman first, then the
  reporter — see the roster for how the lanes are recovered.
- **Content warnings:** four deaths by asphyxiation, described clinically; **a
  child among the dead**; industrial pollution and the working conditions that
  go with it; a man who has chosen five hundred livelihoods over four lives.
- **Art Style:** Ink sketch / pencil illustration. Direction: **fog as a
  subject.** Gaslight haloes, a lantern that makes things worse, chimneys,
  marsh water, a map with pins in it. **The thing is never depicted.**

### Design intent (read this first)

1. **Mobile, and deliberately so.** The convention has three other Gaslight
   games and every one is a single room or building. **This one is the city**:
   hansoms, the river, rooftops, four districts in a night. Forty minutes in
   one location means it has gone wrong.
2. **The rule is findable and the rule is the game.** It travels in thick air
   and is stranded in clear air. Once the players have that, the scenario stops
   being a mystery and becomes **a calculation** — *where will it be when the
   fog lifts?* — which is a far better climax than a chase.
3. **Opens on a body, pitches in one line.** No briefing scene, no hiring, no
   exposition. *(The outline justified this by the game opening the convention
   at 9am. **It no longer does** — the slot moved to Thursday evening on
   2026-10-25, which a night-time fog hunt wanted anyway. The cold open stays,
   because it is right for its own reasons: the scenario's first act is a map
   and it needs the table drawing on it inside five minutes.)*
4. **It has no malice.** It drifts. The deaths are incidental, which is worse
   than if they were not.
5. **The human antagonist is sympathetic and wrong.** Hollis has been
   containing this for three years and has saved more lives than he has cost.
   **The scenario never lets the players feel clean about what they do to him.**

## Plot Summary (GM truth — players never see this page)

**Hollis & Son**, a coal-tar and aniline works on the River Lea, ran a new
process in the autumn of 1893. What came off the retorts that November was not
in any book and has never been repeated. **Benjamin Hollis**, then 51, lost two
men in a night and spent the following week discovering three things:

1. **It is diffuse** — closer to a weather than an animal. There is nothing to
   shoot.
2. **It can only hold together and move where the air is thick enough to carry
   it.** Fog, smoke, steam. In clear air it disperses to something inert and
   **stranded**: it settles where it stood and waits, for days or months, until
   the air comes back.
3. **It can be steered**, crudely, by someone who controls a large chimney.
   Fire the stacks and the air thickens; bank them and it thins.

Hollis has been doing exactly that for three years, pushing it out over the
marshes and the river and away from streets with people in them, writing off
the occasional death as bronchitis in a bad winter.

He has, by any honest reckoning, **saved a great many lives.** He has also
never told anyone, because the works employs five hundred and twelve men and
the day this becomes known is the day it closes.

**Why it is going wrong now.** Hollis is 54 and ill. His son **Edward**, 26,
has been running the works since September, has doubled the output, and fires
the stacks on a **production schedule rather than a weather one.** He does not
know what he is steering. He thinks his father's rules about the chimneys are
an old man's superstition. Four deaths in four weeks is the result.

**Tonight.** The forecast fog is general and will not lift until first light.
The thing will have the whole basin to drift through and will go wherever the
thickest air takes it — which, because Edward has the stacks running for a
Thursday delivery, is **inward, up the river, towards the City.**

**The window.** When the fog lifts, it strands. In clear air, for perhaps six
hours, it is a locatable, inert, vulnerable thing lying in whatever yard or
alley or stretch of water it was over at dawn.

**Dealing with it** needs the works. The only thing that touched it in 1893 was
a reagent off Hollis's own process, and he has **three carboys of it and the
only copy of the method.**

### Revelations (GM checklist)

1. Four deaths in four weeks, no wound, lungs full of something that is not soot.
2. Nothing connects the four — not district, trade, hour or acquaintance.
3. **Each died exactly where the fog lay thickest that night.**
4. The Meteorological Office keeps a register that proves it.
5. Tonight's forecast is the worst of the winter, general, and will not lift
   before dawn.
6. It moves only in thick air and is inert in clear air.
7. **In clear air it strands** — it stops where it was and stays.
8. Hollis & Son's stacks thicken the air downwind and can steer it.
9. Benjamin Hollis has been steering it deliberately for three years.
10. Edward fires the stacks on a delivery schedule and does not believe any of it.
11. The works employs five hundred and twelve men.
12. A reagent from the 1893 process is the only thing that ever touched it, and
    Benjamin has three carboys and the only method.
13. **Where it will strand at first light is calculable** from the register, the
    wind and the stack schedule.

**Undelivered by Act Three?** Benjamin states 6, 7, 8, 9 and 12 in one speech —
see his pacing lever. Act Three has **no new information**.

## Player Experience

Four people have died in four weeks, each on a night of thick fog, each found
in the open street with no wound on them and their lungs full of something that
is not soot. There is nothing to connect the four — not district, not trade,
not hour. Mary Pargeter died in Bow. A lighterman died off Wapping Stairs. A
boy of nine died in Limehouse Causeway. A clerk died on Blackfriars Bridge.

The Meteorological Office keeps a fog register, and the fog register connects
them perfectly. Each of them died exactly where the fog lay thickest that night.

Tonight's forecast is the worst of the winter. By eight it will be from the
marshes to Westminster and it will not lift before dawn — and whatever moves in
it will have the whole of London to move through, and at first light it will
stop wherever it happens to be. For a few hours in clear air it can be found.

Then you wait for the next fog, and the next four.

## ACT TWO — *The Running Night* *(~75 minutes)*

**Shape:** the long act and the mobile one. The fog comes down and the players
are out in it — the Lea marshes, the works, the river, and a pursuit on foot
through Limehouse where the thing is somewhere in the street with them and
cannot be seen, because nothing can.

**Splits 3+2** (3+3 at six, 2+2 at four):

- **Track A — the works.** **Benjamin** Hollis, the process, the reagent, three
  years of silence.
- **Track B — the river and the air.** The Met readings, the stacks' plume,
  the night's movement — **and Edward**, who is at the wharf on the river side
  signing barges in for the Thursday delivery.

**The two Hollises are deliberately on opposite tracks.** That is the point of
the split: Track A spends the act being argued at by a dying man who is mostly
right, and Track B meets the twenty-six-year-old who does not believe a word of
it and is loading the boats anyway. **When the tracks come back together, that
is the scene where Edward is made to believe — in front of his father**, which
is the staging the scenario has always wanted and never scheduled. Schedule it:
**the reunion is a beat, at about 2:15, and it is the act's last one.**

**Neither half can do the Act Three sum alone.** Track A learns the rule and
the reagent; Track B learns tonight's wind, the stacks' schedule, and where the
fog is actually lying — **so Track B arrives at Act Three holding an answer,
not homework.** Alternate in ~10-minute segments and end each on a pull.

### Track A — Hollis & Son

A coal-tar and aniline works on the Lea: retort house, benzol sheds, a cooperage
yard, and two chimneys eighty feet high. Five hundred and twelve men on the
books. At night, a skeleton shift and a gatekeeper who was told not to let
anybody in.

**Benjamin Hollis, 54** — *wants to hand the chimneys to someone who
understands them before he dies.*

Ill — visibly, with the cough the works gave him — exhausted, and **entirely
unrepentant** about three years of silence. He is also right more often than
the players will want him to be. He does not deny anything once he sees they
have the register; he is relieved, and the relief is the most upsetting thing
about him.

> *"I've kept it off the streets for three years with nothing but a stoker's
> rota and a weather glass. Four in four weeks, yes. Before that, three in
> three years, and I know every one of their names and you don't know any of
> them, and that is because I was good at it. Now tell me what you'd have done,
> and mind you answer the part about the five hundred men."*

**His three rules**, given plainly if asked (Revelations 6, 7, 8):
1. It moves only where the air is thick.
2. In clear air it stops, and stays stopped, where it was.
3. A big enough chimney steers it.

**His rule of thumb**, which is the Act Three sum in one sentence:
> *"It goes where the air is thickest. It drifts about half as fast as the wind
> does. And when the air thins it stops — and the air thins last where the
> walls are highest."*

**Edward is not here.** He is on Track B, at the wharf. See below — and bring
them together at 2:15.

**Pacing lever:** if the clock has beaten the table, Benjamin says all three
rules, the schedule and the reagent in one ninety-second speech. He has been
waiting three years for someone to make him say it.

### Track B — the river and the air

The Thames at night in a general fog: a police launch, the Lea's mouth, the
marshes, and readings taken at stations across the basin.

- `[CoC: Natural World or Science (Meteorology) — tonight's wind is light from
  the east-north-east, backing north before dawn]`
- `[CoC: Navigate or Pilot (Boat) — the river is the only place in London where
  you can see the shape of the fog from outside it]`
- `[CoC: Spot Hidden — from the water, at 10pm, the works' plume is visible
  joining the fog and thickening it inward, up-river]`

**The best image in the act:** from a launch in midstream, the fog over the
river is a *surface*, and the plume from two eighty-foot chimneys goes into it
like milk into tea, and the whole mass is moving west.

**Edward Hollis, 26, at the wharf** — *wants a profitable quarter.* He is on
the river side with a tally board and a lamp, signing barges in, and he is
delighted to tell anybody who asks that the stacks went up at ten tonight
because there is a delivery to make. **He does not believe a word of his
father's weather nonsense**, says so cheerfully, and will say it again in front
of his father at the reunion. He is the reason four people are dead, he is not
malicious, and he is twenty-six.

> *"Three years of it. Rotas and a weather glass and 'don't fire the north
> stack, Edward'. Do you know what a quarter's late delivery costs? He's a
> frightened old man with a bad chest and I love him and he is wrong."*

**The line that stops the scenario being an argument** — say it at the
reunion, whatever anybody has promised anybody:
> *"Say I agree with every word. Say I shut the north stack tomorrow. I'll be
> back on it inside a year, because the alternative is five hundred men on the
> street, and you'll all have gone home by then."*

**Track B's own conclusion — watch where it is thickest.** Any table on the
water at four in the morning can see the answer rather than compute it. This is
route 3 to the Act Three sum and it belongs to Track B: **clue 20**, below.

### THE SET-PIECE: THE LIMEHOUSE PURSUIT — GM quick-reference

*Roughly 1am, at the thickest of it. Its own page; run it at the table from
here.*

**The problem it solves:** making a thing frightening when nothing is visible
and nothing is chasing anybody.

**The four rules of the scene, say them to yourself first:**
1. **It is not pursuing them.** It is drifting. Every description must be of
   *the street*, never of a thing approaching.
2. **Lanterns make it worse.** A bullseye lantern in a London particular
   returns a wall of yellow at two feet. Taking the lantern *away* increases
   visibility. Let a player discover this; it is the best moment in the scene.
3. **The only sense that works is hearing**, and fog deadens that too — so
   sound arrives flat, close, and without direction.
4. **Nobody rolls to find it.** `[CoC: no roll locates it. It is located by
   somebody choosing to stand still and stop breathing.]`

**Running the scene — a four-stage pressure, not a chase:**

| Stage | What the players get | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Separation** | Limehouse Causeway, under ten feet of visibility. Within ninety seconds the party is in two or three pieces and cannot reliably rejoin | Say which PCs are together. Keep that list visible |
| **2. The flat sound** | Somewhere, a dog stops. Then a horse, further off, screams and keeps screaming, and then does not | `[CoC: Listen — it is north of you and it is low down]` |
| **3. The cold line** | A PC walks through a band of air perhaps six feet across that is markedly colder and tastes of the works | **Sanity 1/1D4.** This is the only contact in the scenario |
| **4. It passes** | The cold line moves west at walking pace, and a street-sweeper fifty yards up the Causeway is found twenty minutes later, alive, having lain flat in a doorway by luck | Nobody can do anything about any of it |

**If a player attacks it:** they discharge a revolver into fog and the fog is
unchanged, and the sound of the shot brings a constable who takes twenty
minutes of the scenario away.

**If a player stands still in the cold line deliberately:** brave, stupid, and
survivable — they take **Sanity 1/1D4** again and gain Revelation 6 in the most
direct way available, because they feel it decide, without malice, to go the
other way.

**Idle players get jobs:** hand them the street. One runs the sweeper, one runs
a cabman who will not move his horse, one runs a woman at an upstairs window
who can see *over* the worst of it.

> **The woman at the window is an NPC whenever nobody is free to play her —
> which at four players is always.** She is the scene's only aerial view and
> the fastest route to the cold line's direction, and **she shouts anyway.**
> Nothing load-bearing in this set-piece is allowed to depend on there being a
> spare player.
>
> **She is also the written fallback for the lantern.** Let a player discover
> rule 2 if they are going to; if stage 1 has gone by and nobody has, she
> leans out and solves it for them:
> > *"Put your lamp out, you daft beggars — you'll see better!"*
>
> Do not volunteer it yourself, and do not let her say it before stage 1 is
> over. The discovery is the best moment in the scene and she is the insurance,
> not the delivery.

as an input — so settle it before the break, out loud, one of two ways.
**Better: Peck copies the relevant fortnight into his own notebook** before the
register goes back, which puts the climax on a document in a player character's
hand and sets up his Epilogue. Otherwise the duty clerk relents on condition it
is returned by Monday, because he wants to go home more than he wants the
ledger.

### Pacing Note — Act Two

**Must be true before Act Three:** the three rules are known; the stack
schedule is known; tonight's wind is known. **That is the sum's three inputs.**

**Cuts, in order — the act is planned at 75 minutes, so expect to take the
first one:**
1. Drop the marshes.
2. **Shorten the pursuit to stages 1, 2 and 3**, and narrate stage 4 in one
   sentence. Stage 2 is the scene's only use of the one sense the scene says
   works; stage 4 is atmosphere.
3. Merge Edward into the reunion only — **do not cut the reunion**, and **do
   not cut Benjamin.**

**Act Two is planned to end at 2:30** — on the reunion — **and at 2:45 you go
to Act Three** whatever is unfinished. Benjamin tells them the answer at 2:25
if the sum is not forming.

## ACT THREE — *First Light* *(~30 minutes)*

**A decision, not a die roll.** `[CoC: no roll resolves Act Three.]`

### THE SUM — GM quick-reference

**Three inputs, one rule of thumb, one answer.** It must be solvable by a table
that has paid attention and **must not become homework.**

| Input | Where it came from | What it says tonight |
|---|---|---|
| **The fog** | `H2-A` + tonight's forecast | General, marshes to Westminster, no lift before dawn |
| **The wind** | Clue 17 | Light from the ENE, backing north before dawn |
| **The stacks** | `H2-B`, clue 13 — **from Track B, via Edward at the wharf** | Fired at ten, thickening the air **inward, up-river, westward** |

**Benjamin's rule of thumb:** *"It goes where the air is thickest. It drifts
about half as fast as the wind does. And when the air thins it stops — and the
air thins last where the walls are highest."*

**The working:** it comes off the marshes on the ENE wind, is drawn and held
westward along the river by the works' plume from ten o'clock, and travels
perhaps three miles in the night. At first light, **open water and open ground
clear first.** The last thick air in its line is enclosed, high-walled and wet.

**The answer: the Limehouse Basin** — enclosed dock water, warehouses on all
four sides, the last place in the district to clear. It strands on the water at
about 7:12am and lies there, inert, for perhaps six hours.

> The basin at first light is flat and black and perfectly still, and lying on
> it, from the coal wharf to the lock gates, is a sheet of something the colour
> of a bruise that is not mist and is not oil and does not move with the water
> underneath it. In clear air, in daylight, with four warehouse walls round it,
> you can see the whole of it at once. It is about an acre.

**Sanity 1/1D3** for everyone who sees it. This is the only time in the
scenario the thing is visible, and it is visible because it is helpless.

**Honour their working.** If the table reasons its way to a *different*
defensible point — a dock cutting, a brewery yard, a railway basin — **it
strands there instead.** Nothing downstream depends on which basin it is, and
rewarding the reasoning is worth more than the pin. Say yes.

**Three routes to the answer, so it cannot be missed:**
1. **The arithmetic** — register, wind, schedule, as above.
2. **The rule of thumb and a map** — Benjamin's sentence plus `H1-A` gets a
   table there in under a minute.
3. **Empirically — clue 20.** Track B watches where the fog is thickest at
   four in the morning from the water and goes there. **A table whose Track B
   did this arrives at Act Three with the answer already named**, and the
   arithmetic becomes the confirmation rather than the hurdle. That is a good
   session, not a spoiled one.

**GM fallback at 2:40:** Benjamin says it. He is ill, he is out of time, and he
would rather someone else knew.

### The decision

| Choice | What it takes | What it costs |
|---|---|---|
| **Destroy it** | Benjamin's reagent and his method — **and therefore a deal that protects the works** | He will give it, on terms. The terms are silence. The players end the thing and become the next people keeping the secret |
| **Leave it** | Benjamin back on the chimneys, Edward removed | Four deaths a winter, five hundred and twelve men in work, **and the players knowing.** The most defensible ending and the hardest to say out loud |
| **Expose the works** | A newspaper, a coroner, or both | The works close. Hollis is ruined and probably hanged. **And the thing is still there, drifting, with nobody's hand on it** |
| **Take the stacks themselves** | Seize or buy the works' firing schedule | Some table will propose it, so it is written: it works, it requires one of them to stay in London fires-and-weather-glass for the rest of their life, and the scenario treats that as the serious offer it is |

**Compromise endings players will invent:**
- **Destroy it and expose them anyway.** Available, and Benjamin saw it coming
  and gives them the reagent regardless, which is worse.
- **Tell Edward and nobody else.** He is twenty-six and he now believes. He is
  also the next Benjamin, for thirty years.
- **Get the Met Office to run the schedule.** Institutional, sane, and it means
  explaining this to a government department in 1896.
- **Buy the works.** The reporter's paper could. The chemist would run it.

### If someone shoots

Nothing can be shot, which is stated in GM Essentials and must be true at the
table. A player who shoots **Edward or Benjamin** gets a real ending: the
chimneys stop being steered by anybody who understands them, and the Epilogue
is *Expose* without the newspaper.

### Pacing Note — Act Three

**Must be true before the Epilogue:** the landing point named; the players
there; the decision said out loud. **If you are behind**, put `H1-A` in the
middle of the table at 2:50 and ask: *"Where is it at dawn, and what are you
going to do when you're standing next to it?"*

## Epilogue — *Bronchitis in a Bad Winter* *(~15 minutes)*

### Outcomes

| Outcome | What it reads like |
|---|---|
| **Destroyed** | Three carboys into still water at eight in the morning, and a reaction that takes four minutes and smells of the works. Nothing dramatic. Afterwards the basin is just a basin. **The works stays open, and the players keep the secret, which was the price** |
| **Left** | Benjamin back on the weather glass, Edward sent to Manchester. The register will show the next one, and the players will read it. Three deaths in the next three years, and they will know each of the names, which Benjamin said they would not |
| **Exposed** | Hollis & Son closes in February. Five hundred and twelve men out of work in the worst winter since '79. Benjamin is committed for trial and dies before it. **And on the next thick night, something comes off the marshes with nobody's hand on it at all** |
| **The stacks taken** | One of them stays. The schedule is kept. It is a life's work and the scenario does not pretend otherwise |
| **Pyrrhic** | A Hollis shot or dead, nobody holding the method | Both the thing and the works are now nobody's problem, which means they are everybody's |

### Per-PC vignettes

- **The Met Office clerk.** His arithmetic was right and nobody will ever be
  able to say so. He has started keeping a second register at home, in his own
  hand, and he does not know what he will do with it.
- **The police surgeon.** She refused to sign a fourth certificate. Whatever
  the table chose, she has to write *something* on the fifth, and the word
  available to her is bronchitis.
- **The river sergeant.** Nineteen years of the Thames and he has been afraid
  of the air since November. He takes the launch out on the next thick night
  because somebody has to, and he is the only person aboard who knows why.
- **The reporter.** He has the story of the decade. Whether he filed it is the
  whole of his character and the table decided it for him.
- **The works chemist.** She spent four years chasing Hollis's 1893 run, and
  now she has the method in her hand. **She is the only person alive who could
  do it again.**
- **The cabman** *(if played)*. He drove five educated people round London all
  night in a fog that killed four people, and he is the only one of them who
  stopped when the horse would not go on.

### Table debrief

One question: **"Was Benjamin wrong?"** Do not answer it.

### Closing Scene (read aloud, adapt)

> It is a clear cold morning over the Pool of London, the way it always is
> after one of these, and the city has entirely forgotten what it was like six
> hours ago. Barges are working. Somebody is selling coffee at the top of the
> stairs. Four miles east, two chimneys at a works on the Lea are either
> smoking or they are not, and whichever it is, five hundred and twelve men
> will come through the gates at six tomorrow morning and none of them will
> ever be told anything at all.

## Open questions — **resolved for v1**

1. **How hard is the Act Three sum?** → **Three inputs, one rule of thumb, and
   three independent routes to the answer**, with a GM fallback at 2:40 where
   Benjamin simply says it. Crucially, **the GM honours a defensible wrong
   answer** — if the table reasons to a different basin, it strands there. The
   sum is a reward for attention, never a gate.
2. **Does the thing get a name or an explanation?** → **Neither.** It came off
   a retort in 1893 and the scenario never says what it is. It is never named
   in narration and never depicted in the art.
3. **A nine-year-old among the four dead.** → **Kept, in backstory only, never
   on screen**, and named in the content warnings. The outline worried about
   this as the convention's 9am opener; **the slot moved to Thursday evening**,
   which makes it easier, and the child is the reason Limehouse carries weight
   the other three deaths do not.
4. **The Limehouse chase must be frightening with nothing visible.** → Written
   as a **four-stage pressure, not a chase**, with four stated rules (it is not
   pursuing them; lanterns make it worse; hearing is the only sense and fog
   flattens it; no roll locates it). **It still wants two playtests, not one.**
5. **Four Gaslight games share a period.** → **Build the handout furniture
   once.** Letterheads, register rules, ruled ledger paper, type and stock are
   common to Slots 1, 3, 6 and 8; only the content differs. This is a
   production task, not a scenario task — it belongs in the print pack.

### New open questions, raised by writing v1

1. **Is Benjamin too persuasive?** **Desk pass 1: probably yes — and the
   author's own fix is now applied.** Edward has the line, at the reunion, in
   front of his father: he will be back on the stacks inside a year whatever
   anybody promises. That turns an argument back into a decision. **Re-test it
   in pass 2**, because moving Edward to Track B means Track A now spends the
   whole act being persuaded by Benjamin with nobody to answer him.
2. **Does the 3+2 split starve Track B?** **Confirmed at desk pass 1 — eight
   clues to two — and fixed by redistribution, not addition.** Edward and the
   stack schedule are Track B's now, and Track B has its own conclusion in
   clue 20. Roughly 6/4. **Measure it again in pass 2.**
3. **Six players in a fog.** The pursuit's first stage deliberately splits the
   party. At six that could become three groups of two and a GM juggling three
   conversations in a scene whose whole point is that nobody can find anybody.
   **Untested at pass 1** (played at four), and note the risk runs the other
   way at six: the street roles get played rather than narrated, so the scene
   is better resourced and the GM is busier. **Flag for pass 3.**

## GM Notes (private)

- **Keep them moving.** The single most likely failure is the table settling
  into the works for forty minutes. If that starts, the fog thickens somewhere
  else and somebody comes to tell them.
- **Benjamin is not a villain and Edward is not a monster.** Benjamin saved
  lives for three years by doing something nobody would sanction. Edward is a
  young man who thinks his ill father is superstitious. Play them both
  straight and let the table be angry at an arrangement rather than a person.
- **The thing has no malice and you must not give it any.** Never describe it
  approaching, choosing, or noticing. It drifts. The deaths are incidental,
  which is worse.
- **The lantern discovery is the best moment in the scenario.** Do not
  volunteer it. Wait for a player to say *"put it out"*, and then make the
  street open up by six feet.
